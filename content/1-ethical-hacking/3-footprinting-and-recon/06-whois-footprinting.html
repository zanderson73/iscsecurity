1. Overview & Threat Landscape

WHOIS (historically TCP/43, now largely superseded by RDAP: Registration Data Access Protocol ) is a query-and-response system that retrieves registration metadata for domain names, IP address blocks, and autonomous systems. In modern operations, adversaries do not simply "look up a domain."

They use WHOIS/RDAP data to:

  • Construct an organisation's external attack surface (domains, subsidiaries, registrar relationships).
  • Identify authoritative name servers to target for DNS hijacking or zone enumeration.
  • Harvest historical contact emails and phone numbers for spear-phishing and vishing pretexts.
  • Map NetRanges to physical data-centre locations, aiding in both logical and physical attack planning.
  • Detect supply-chain dependencies (e.g., a target using a niche registrar or DNS provider may share credentials across platforms).

Because GDPR and ICANN privacy policies have heavily redacted contemporary WHOIS records, sophisticated Red Teams now pivot to historical WHOIS archives, RDAP entity handles, and RIR point-of-contact (POC) databases that often remain exposed even when the registrar record is cloaked.

BlueOps Note:
Every piece of WHOIS data an attacker can see is an exposed seam. Registrar compromise remains a top-10 initial-access vector because it grants global DNS control without ever touching the target's firewall.

2. Adversarial Methodology: Phased Recon

  1. Phase 1: Baseline Domain Enumeration: Identify primary, subsidiary, and parked domains via certificate transparency logs, DNS brute-forcing, and search-engine dorking. Feed confirmed domains into WHOIS/RDAP.
  2. Phase 2: Current-State Registration Harvesting: Query live RDAP/WHOIS for registrar, status flags, name servers, and creation/expiry dates. Document redactions and privacy shields.
  3. Phase 3: Historical Registration Pivoting: Query historical WHOIS services (e.g., WHOISXML API, DomainTools, ViewDNS.info) for pre-GDPR contact data, previous registrars, and infrastructure drift.
  4. Phase 4: Network Range & RIR Correlation: Pivot from resolved IPs to RIR allocations (ARIN, RIPE, APNIC, LACNIC, AFRINIC). Extract NetRange, CIDR, ASN, and abuse contacts.
  5. Phase 5: Geolocation & Infrastructure Mapping: Map IP blocks to cities, data-centre regions, and cloud providers to infer likely hosting regions and latency-based failover architecture.
  6. Phase 6: Weaponisation Prep: Correlate discovered abuse/security emails, outdated name servers, or transfer-unlock statuses into phishing pretexts or domain-transfer attacks.

4. Whois Data Models & Regional Internet Registries (RIRs)

Understand the data architecture before you query:

  • Thick WHOIS: Central registry holds the complete record (e.g., legacy .ORG). Useful for direct bulk queries.
  • Thin WHOIS: Central registry only references the registrar; full data lives at the registrar. Most gTLDs (.com, .net) now use this model.
  • RDAP (JSON over HTTPS): The modern replacement for WHOIS. Returns structured JSON, supports authentication/authorisation, and is easier to parse at scale.
  • Decentralised WHOIS / RIR Databases: IP allocation and ASN data are maintained independently by the five RIRs.

5. Typical Fields & Capture Strategy

Whether querying RDAP or classic WHOIS, systematically capture:

  • Domain / Handle: Unique object identifier (e.g., 2724960_DOMAIN_COM-VRSN).
  • Registrar & IANA ID: The managing entity and its numeric ID. Useful for supply-chain targeting.
  • Status Flags: clientTransferProhibited, clientDeleteProhibited, serverHold, etc. A missing transfer lock is a vulnerability.
  • Event Dates: Registration, expiration, last changed. Expiry proximity indicates potential domain-squatting or ransom risk.
  • Name Servers: Authoritative DNS hosts. Outsourced NS (e.g., Azure-DNS, Cloudflare) reveals cloud architecture.
  • Entities / vCards: In RDAP, structured contact data. Even "redacted" records expose abuse desks and registrar URLs.
  • NetRange / CIDR / Origin AS: From RIR IP lookups. Defines the network perimeter.
Evidence Protocol:
Save raw JSON (RDAP) or text (WHOIS) alongside a UTC timestamp and source URL. Parsed views can drop critical notices (e.g., redaction disclaimers, terms-of-use violations).

8. Case Studies

Case Study 1: Sea Turtle/DNSpionage; Registrar Compromise via WHOIS Pivoting

Between 2017 and 2019, the Sea Turtle APT group targeted telecoms and government entities not by breaching the victims directly, but by compromising their registrars and DNS providers. They harvested WHOIS data to identify administrative contact emails at smaller, less-hardened registrars. Using spear-phishing against those contacts, they obtained registrar panel credentials, modified NS records to attacker-controlled resolvers, and redirected VPN and webmail traffic to steal credentials. The initial pivot was purely WHOIS-derived: knowing who managed the DNS and where to send the phish.

Registrar and DNS provider contacts are high-value targets. Blue teams must treat registrar credentials with the same protection as domain-admin accounts (MFA, registry lock, out-of-band change verification).

Case Study 2: GDPR Redaction Bypass via Historical WHOIS

After the EU GDPR came into force in 2018, many commercial registrars redacted registrant names, emails, and phone numbers from public WHOIS. However, historical WHOIS archives (e.g., DomainTools, WHOISXML API, SecurityTrails) retained pre-2018 records. In multiple penetration-test engagements, Red Teams successfully obtained the personal mobile numbers and Gmail addresses of C-suite executives from 2016-era WHOIS records of since-transferred domains. These numbers were then used for SIM-swap pretexting and WhatsApp phishing.

Privacy redaction on current records does not erase history. Blue teams should audit historical WHOIS exposure and request redaction retroactively where possible.

Case Study 3: APT41: ASN & NetRange Mapping for Supply-Chain Targeting

APT41 (Barium / Winnti) is known to map the NetRanges of gaming and technology firms via APNIC and RIPE queries. By identifying overlapping IP blocks between a primary target and its smaller vendors, they targeted the less-hardened vendor first, then hopped into the primary target via VPN or software-update channels. WHOIS-derived ASN data revealed which IP ranges belonged to the same corporate entity versus third-party hosting.

NetRange correlation across subsidiaries and vendors is a supply-chain reconnaissance vector. Blue teams should monitor ASN-level changes and unexpected routing announcements.

9. OPSEC & Legal Boundaries

9.1 Operational Security for Red Teams

  • Rate Limiting & Throttling: RDAP services explicitly reject bulk queries from single IPs. Space out requests; use rotating residential proxies if querying at scale.
  • Query Attribution: WHOIS/RDAP queries are logged by registries and may be shared with domain owners under abuse investigations. Use a non-attributable IP and VM.
  • Historical Data Hygiene: Historical WHOIS services may require paid API keys. Do not leak client target lists by uploading bulk domains to untrusted third-party web forms.
  • Terms of Service: Automated scraping of registrar RDAP endpoints may violate ICANN or registrar ToS. Adhere to engagement scope and acceptable-use policies.

9.2 Legal Boundaries

Jurisdiction / Framework Relevant Constraint Risk to Operator
GDPR / EDPB Guidelines Automated collection and processing of personal data from WHOIS (even if public) must have a lawful basis. Storing PII without justification may breach data-protection laws. Fines up to 4% global turnover; civil liability; investigation by DPA.
ICANN RDAP Terms Bulk query restrictions; data mining prohibitions; requirement to use data only for lawful purposes. IP blacklisting; referral to law enforcement; civil action by registry.
CFAA / Computer Misuse While passive WHOIS lookup is generally lawful, circumventing rate limits or authentication gates to scrape RDAP may constitute unauthorised access. Criminal prosecution; loss of professional certifications.
Golden Rule (ROE):
If you are not certain the activity is within signed Rules of Engagement (RoE) and legal review, do not execute it. When in doubt, use only passive, unauthenticated techniques.

10. Quick Triaging Checklist: WHOIS Recon Workflow

□ Scope & Authorisation
    □ Client explicitly authorises WHOIS/RDAP reconnaissance.
    □ List of target domains, IPs, and ASNs defined.
    □ Agreement on whether historical WHOIS is in scope.

□ Infrastructure Setup
    □ Non-attributable VM / cloud workstation ready.
    □ Proxy / VPN configured to avoid IP throttling.
    □ API keys provisioned for historical WHOIS services (if needed).

□ Phase 1 — Domain Baseline
    □ Resolve each domain to current IPv4/IPv6 (dig / host).
    □ Query live RDAP / WHOIS for registrar, status, dates, NS.
    □ Screenshot or save raw JSON/text output with UTC timestamp.

□ Phase 2 — Historical Pivot
    □ Query historical WHOIS for pre-redaction contacts.
    □ Check DomainTools, WHOISXML API, SecurityTrails, ViewDNS.
    □ Note any previous registrars, name servers, or country changes.

□ Phase 3 — RIR & Network Enumeration
    □ Run RIR WHOIS for resolved IPs (ARIN, RIPE, APNIC, LACNIC, AFRINIC).
    □ Extract NetRange, CIDR, Origin AS, OrgAbuse contacts.
    □ Perform reverse-DNS (PTR) lookups for the NetRange.

□ Phase 4 — Geolocation & Context
    □ Geolocate primary IPs (city, region, timezone, ISP/org).
    □ Map to cloud regions (e.g., AWS us-east-1).
    □ Correlate with physical office locations for travel / vishing context.

□ Phase 5 — Risk Analysis & Weaponisation Prep
    □ Flag missing transfer locks, expired certificates, or DNSSEC = false.
    □ Identify shared registrars between target and weaker subsidiaries.
    □ Build phishing pretexts only if authorised under a social-engineering workstream.

□ Phase 6 — Blue-Team Handoff
    □ Generate Indicators of Exposure (IoE): exposed POCs, weak status flags, historical PII.
    □ Recommend defensive mitigations (Registry Lock, DNSSEC, privacy proxy).
    □ Securely purge raw PII not required for the risk narrative.

12. Defensive Countermeasures & Threat Mapping

12.1 Blue Team Mitigations

  • Registry Lock: Enable serverDeleteProhibited, serverTransferProhibited, and serverUpdateProhibited at the registry level
    (not just registrar). This prevents unauthorised changes even if the registrar account is compromised.
  • DNSSEC: Sign your zones. While not a panacea, DNSSEC prevents trivial NS takeover and cache-poisoning attacks that often follow registrar reconnaissance.
  • Privacy / Proxy Registration: Use WHOIS privacy services for all non-corporate domains. For corporate domains, use a role-based email
    (e.g., domain-admin@company.com) rather than a personal inbox.
  • Historical Data Scrubbing: Contact historical WHOIS providers and request deletion of pre-GDPR personal data. Monitor for reappearance.
  • Registrar Account Hardening: MFA on registrar panels (hardware keys preferred); IP allow-listing for admin access; out-of-band email confirmation for EPP/code changes.
  • ASN & BGP Monitoring: Subscribe to BGP anomaly alerts (e.g., from BGPStream or ThousandEyes) to detect hijacks of your NetRanges.
  • Phishing Simulation: Specifically test staff who hold registrar/RIR POC roles. They are high-value targets and often overlooked in security awareness.

Blue Team Note:
Indirect intelligence sources are where organisations have the least visibility but the most exposure. You cannot firewall RDAP or block WHOIS queries, but you can control what those services reveal. Data hygiene is perimeter defence for the OSINT age.

13. Ethical Considerations & Engagement Scope

  • Authorisation: WHOIS lookups are passive, but bulk querying or using paid historical databases must be explicitly authorised in the RoE.
  • Data Minimisation: Do not retain personal contact details (phones, home addresses) extracted from historical WHOIS unless directly relevant to demonstrating a critical risk.
  • No Registrar Interference: Never attempt to log in to a target's registrar panel, request transfer codes, or socially engineer registrar staff unless that exact vector is pre-approved.
  • Responsible Disclosure: If you discover that a client's domain lacks Registry Lock or DNSSEC, treat it as a vulnerability and report it through the defined disclosure channel.

17. Knowledge Check

Reflection Questions

  1. A WHOIS query reveals that your target's domain status includes clientTransferProhibited but not serverTransferProhibited.
    What is the practical difference between these flags, and why is the absence of the server flag a critical finding?
    Consider who enforces each lock (registrar vs. registry) and the implications for domain hijacking resilience.
  2. Historical WHOIS records show that the target's CTO registered their corporate domain using their personal Gmail address and mobile number in 2014. How can this finding be weaponised in a social-engineering campaign, and what defensive measures should the organisation take?
    Think about password reset attacks, spear-phishing, SIM-swapping, and the role of WHOIS privacy services.
  3. You discover that a target's NetRange is allocated to a cloud provider in us-east-1. What specific AWS services might be in scope for further reconnaissance, and how would you pivot from this information?
    Consider EC2, S3, RDS, and the use of cloud-specific OSINT tools like cloudbrute or bucket enumeration.
  4. An organisation uses a niche registrar that has a history of poor security practices. How does this increase supply-chain risk, and what alternatives should the Blue Team consider?
    Think about registrar compromise as an initial-access vector and the benefits of Registry Lock / DNSSEC.
  5. Why is RDAP considered an improvement over WHOIS from both a technical and a privacy perspective, and how does this affect adversarial reconnaissance?
    Consider structured JSON responses, role-based access control, and the impact on bulk enumeration.

Scenario-Based Question

Scenario: You are conducting a Red Team assessment against Fabricated Industries, a multinational manufacturing conglomerate. Your Rules of Engagement permit passive WHOIS/RDAP reconnaissance and limited historical data collection. Initial open-source research has identified the primary domain fabricatedindustries.com, several subsidiary domains, and a recently acquired company legacy-supply.com.

Task: Design a comprehensive WHOIS/RDAP reconnaissance workflow to map the organisation's domain portfolio, identify infrastructure dependencies, uncover historical PII exposure, and prioritise registrar-level vulnerabilities for reporting to the Blue Team.

Suggested Approach:
  • Phase 1: Domain Portfolio Discovery: Enumerate all domains associated with Fabricated Industries using certificate transparency logs (crt.sh), DNS brute-forcing, and search-engine dorking. Identify primary, subsidiary, and parked domains.
  • Phase 2: Current-State RDAP/WHOIS Harvesting: Query live RDAP for each domain to extract registrar, status flags (clientTransferProhibited, serverTransferProhibited), name servers, and creation/expiry dates. Document redactions and privacy shields.
  • Phase 3: Historical Registration Pivoting: Query historical WHOIS services (DomainTools, WHOISXML API, ViewDNS.info) for pre-GDPR contact data, previous registrars, and infrastructure drift. Look for personal email addresses, phone numbers, and physical addresses.
  • Phase 4: RIR NetRange & ASN Mapping: Resolve each domain to IP addresses and pivot to RIR databases (ARIN, RIPE, APNIC). Extract NetRange, CIDR blocks, Origin AS, and abuse contacts. Identify cloud providers and data-centre regions.
