1. Module Introduction: Mapping the Battlefield

Before an ethical hacker sends a single packet or an adversary launches a single phishing campaign, there is a phase of quiet, structured information gathering. In military terms, this is intelligence preparation of the battlefield. In cybersecurity, we call it footprinting and reconnaissance.

Footprinting is the disciplined process of collecting publicly available data about a target organization, its people, and its technology stack. When done well, it produces a precise map that makes every subsequent phase; scanning, exploitation, lateral movement, and reporting more accurate, efficient, and defensible.

In this module, you will learn reconnaissance from two perspectives simultaneously: the adversary who weaponizes public data, and the defender who must harden that same attack surface. Each section pairs real-world case studies with hands-on toolchains, legal guardrails, and defensive countermeasures.

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

2. Real-World Threat Context

The techniques in this module are not theoretical. The case studies below illustrate how modern adversaries from ransomware gangs to nation-state actors, invest weeks in reconnaissance before their first malicious action. Understanding their workflow is essential to replicating it ethically and defending against it effectively.

Case Study 1: The Hospitality Heist: MGM / Caesars (2023)

The intrusion group Scattered Spider spent weeks mapping MGM and Caesars employees on LinkedIn. By identifying IT helpdesk staff and their reporting chains, the attackers impersonated employees via voice calls (vishing) to reset MFA tokens and VPN access. The breach cost MGM over $100 million in operational disruption.

Learning Point: Professional-network reconnaissance directly enables social-engineering initial access. The weakest link is often the human, not the perimeter.

Case Study 2: The Cloud Data Tsunami: Snowflake (2024)

Threat actors (UNC5537) used prior stealer-log intelligence and tenant recon to identify accounts lacking MFA, specifically contractor and service accounts. They exfiltrated data from ~165 customers including Ticketmaster and Santander. The attack was surgical because the adversary knew exactly which tenants were soft targets before authentication.

Learning Point: Identity reconnaissance + breach-data cross-correlation = mass cloud compromise without vulnerability exploitation.

Case Study 3: The Tenant Trust Pivot: Midnight Blizzard / SVR (2024–2025)

Russian state actors performed sustained reconnaissance against Microsoft 365 tenants. By enumerating public Entra ID endpoints and OAuth app registrations, they identified trust relationships between organizations and Microsoft services. They then created malicious OAuth applications and tricked low-privilege users into granting consent pivoting from recon to persistent cloud access.

Learning Point: Cloud configuration metadata is a first-class intelligence target. Trust relationships are the new attack surface.

Case Study 4: AI-Enhanced Social Engineering (2025–2026)

Adversaries now deploy LLM pipelines to ingest earnings calls, SEC filings, job postings (which enumerate EDR/SIEM versions) and GitHub commit histories. This produces synthetic org charts, technology stack maps, and cloned executive voices. In 2025, a fintech firm was compromised after attackers used AI-generated audio trained on public conference recordings to authorize a fraudulent wire transfer.

Learning Point: AI scales OSINT from "manual browsing" to automated corporate intelligence at nation-state fidelity.

Case Study 5: The Staging Environment Goldmine (Ongoing)

Certificate Transparency logs and passive DNS routinely expose staging-api, dev-admin, and legacy-backup subdomains. These endpoints often lack WAFs, run outdated builds, and use weaker authentication. Multiple 2025 ransomware incidents traced initial access to a forgotten Jenkins instance discovered via subdomain enumeration.

Learning Point: Production is hardened; staging and dev are where adversaries live for months undetected.

3. TTP-1: Passive OSINT & Search Engine Reconnaissance

Concept: Passive footprinting generates no packets to the target's infrastructure. The investigator relies entirely on third-party indexes, cached content, and public archives. This is the safest phase for operators and the hardest for defenders to detect.

3.1 Search Engine Dorking

Search engines index far more than web pages. PDFs, spreadsheets, API documentation, and configuration files often leak internal usernames, software versions, or network diagrams. Advanced search operators, sometimes called Google dorks , allow precise targeting of these exposures.

  • File-type harvesting: Query for exposed documents that reveal internal structure.
  • Cache evasion: Use archived and cached sources to retrieve documents the target thought they deleted.
  • Job posting analysis: "Hiring SOC Analyst with CrowdStrike Falcon v7" tells an adversary exactly which EDR evasion toolkit to bring.
# Identify exposed documents via search engine recon
web_search --query "site:target.com filetype:pdf intitle:confidential"

# Find exposed API documentation or swagger endpoints
web_search --query "site:target.com (swagger OR api-docs OR openapi) (json OR yaml)"

# Identify technology stack leakage via job postings
web_search --query "\"Target Corp\" ( CrowdStrike OR SentinelOne OR Splunk ) (engineer OR analyst)"
Check Your Understanding:
Why might searching for filetype:pdf site:target.com reveal more sensitive information than a standard Google search of the target's homepage?

3.2 Code Repository & Paste Site Recon

Developers frequently commit hardcoded credentials, internal hostnames, and infrastructure-as-code templates. Paste sites serve as temporary drop-zones for breached credentials or troubleshooting output before formal leak databases index them. 

# Search for exposed internal hostnames or credentials in public repos
web_search --query "site:github.com \"target.com\" (password OR api_key OR secret) extension:yml"

# Direct fetch of a suspicious paste (if URL is known)
curl --url "https://pastebin.com/raw/XXXXXX"

3.3 Metadata & Image Intelligence

Images and documents carry hidden metadata. A press-release photo may contain GPS coordinates. A PDF's author field may reveal an internal Active Directory username. Understanding how to extract and interpret this data is a core OSINT skill. 

# Extract EXIF from a leaked corporate image
image_exif --url "https://target.com/press/photo.jpg"
OPSEC Principle:
Always query search engines and archival services through engagement-specific proxies or burner infrastructure. Your Google account, IP address, and browser fingerprint are logged and correlated.

4. TTP-2: Technical Infrastructure Reconnaissance

Concept: Once you know who and what, you must map where it lives. This phase transitions from purely passive OSINT to semi-active reconnaissance: DNS queries, certificate lookups, and service fingerprinting. In many jurisdictions, these activities occupy a legal grey zone unless explicitly authorized in your Rules of Engagement.

4.1 Domain & Registration Intelligence (WHOIS / RDAP)

WHOIS and RDAP (Registration Data Access Protocol) reveal registration details, name servers, and historical contacts. Even when privacy protection is enabled, historical records, DNS delegations, and transfer histories frequently leak organizational pivots. 

# Retrieve current registration and name-server details
whois --domain target.com

# Cross-reference historical registrar contacts for pivoting
web_search --query "target.com historical whois registrant email"

4.2 DNS Resolution & Geolocation Mapping

Mapping domains to IPs reveals hosting providers, CDN usage, and geographic concentration of assets. IP geolocation informs physical social engineering, legal jurisdiction selection, and attack-path planning. 

# Resolve domain to IP
get_domain_ip --domain target.com

# Map IP geolocation for infrastructure targeting
get_ip_geolocation --ip_address "203.0.113.45"

4.3 Subdomain Enumeration & Certificate Transparency

Subdomains represent the largest expansion of attack surface. Names like dev, staging, vpn, and backup frequently host weaker authentication or older software. Certificate Transparency logs are passively harvested and cannot be disabled by defenders without breaking TLS. 

# Enumerate subdomains via passive DNS aggregation
subfinder --target_domain target.com

# Reference external CT log search (conceptual link)
# https://crt.sh/?q=%.target.com

4.4 SSL/TLS & Service Fingerprinting

TLS configuration exposes legacy protocols, cipher-suite preferences, and certificate SANs that may reveal internal service names. Web fingerprinting identifies server software, frameworks, and missing security headers critical for tailoring exploit selection. 

# Analyze TLS configuration and supported cipher suites
sslscan --target_domain target.com

# Identify server software, frameworks, and security headers
web_fingerprint --host target.com
Check Your Understanding:
A subfinder scan reveals legacy-api.target.com resolving to an IP in a different country than the main website. What risks might this subdomain present, and why would defenders have difficulty monitoring it?

5. TTP-3: Human Attack Surface & Identity Reconnaissance

Concept: The modern perimeter is identity. Adversaries invest heavily in mapping employees, contractors, and organizational hierarchies because a single compromised identity bypasses firewalls, EDR, and network segmentation. As an ethical operator, you must understand how to perform this mapping and when doing so requires explicit written authorization.

5.1 Email Enumeration & Pattern Analysis

Valid email lists enable password spraying, spear-phishing, and MFA fatigue attacks. Common patterns (e.g., firstname.lastname) can be extrapolated from a single leaked address. 

# Harvest valid corporate emails via public sources and APIs
emailbook --domain target.com --max_results 200

# Validate whether harvested emails have breach exposure
leaks_search --query "user@target.com"

5.2 Social Engineering Considerations

  • Requires Explicit Authorization: Any interaction with target personnel; email, phone, social media outreach, or physical pretexting must be covered by written RoE. Unsolicited contact may violate anti-stalking, fraud, or computer-misuse laws.
  • Professional Network Mapping: LinkedIn, conference speaker lists, and certification databases build trust profiles and reveal internal team structures.
  • AI-Enhanced Social Engineering: LLMs generate context-aware phishing lures based on recent GitHub commits or internal jargon discovered during recon.
OPSEC Principle:
If you interact with a target employee using a synthetic identity, assume that interaction is preserved indefinitely. Burner accounts must be fully isolated; no shared IP, device, or payment method with your real identity.
Check Your Understanding:
During reconnaissance, you discover a Help Desk manager's email via LinkedIn. You also find their password in a 2023 breach dump. Under what conditions is it legally and ethically permissible to attempt authentication with these credentials?

6. OPSEC & Legal Boundaries for Reconnaissance

Ethical footprinting requires a clear understanding of where reconnaissance ends and unauthorized access begins. This section establishes the guardrails that keep your investigation lawful, scoped, and defensible.

6.1 The Passive vs. Active Boundary

  • Passive OSINT (search engines, public records, CT logs) is generally lawful in most jurisdictions but may violate platform Terms of Service.
  • Semi-Active Recon (DNS queries, WHOIS lookups, subfinder) often leaves logs at third-party resolvers but typically does not alert the target directly.
  • Active Scanning (port scans, vulnerability probes) crosses into unauthorized access territory under laws like the U.S. CFAA and UK Computer Misuse Act unless explicitly authorized.

6.2 Attribution Controls

  • Proxy Isolation: Route all automated recon through residential or engagement-specific proxies. Corporate egress IPs link your organization directly to target intelligence gathering.
  • Rate Limiting & Rotation: Aggressive subdomain enumeration or search-engine scraping triggers CAPTCHA and IP bans. Rotate User-Agents and throttle requests.
  • API Key Segregation: Paid recon APIs should be registered with burner identities and non-attributional payment.
# Route tool queries through engagement proxy
export HTTPS_PROXY=http://engagement-proxy:3128
subfinder --target_domain target.com

# Rotate Tor circuit between target queries
torsocks subfinder --target_domain target.com

6.3 Data Hygiene for Recon Artifacts

  • Store recon notes, screenshots, and structured output (JSON, CSV) in an encrypted container.
  • Segregate recon data by engagement. Never retain employee PII, family photos, or out-of-scope corporate documents after the engagement concludes.

7. Quick Triaging Checklist

  • Scope relevance: Does the discovered asset (subdomain, email, credential, document) belong to the in-scope target or a third party? If out of scope, cease collection immediately.
  • Freshness vs. staleness: Prioritize recently discovered subdomains, live stealer-log credentials, and active job postings over archived data from >2 years ago.
  • Cross-verification: Confirm critical findings across at least two independent sources before reporting or weaponizing.
  • Synthetic dump check: Validate that breached credential lists are not AI-generated synthetic noise. Look for consistent hash types, realistic password complexity, and corroborating breach sources.
  • Document provenance: Record the exact query, timestamp, tool version, and source URL for every artifact. Chain of custody matters in court and in client reporting.

9. Defensive Countermeasures & Threat Mapping

Defenders cannot prevent adversaries from reading public data, but they can increase the cost of reconnaissance and detect the transition from recon to exploitation.

Defensive Best Practices

  • Attack Surface Reduction: Remove stale subdomains, expired certificates, and orphaned DNS records. If it resolves, it will be found.
  • Employee Exposure Minimization: Limit detailed org charts on public websites; use role-based email aliases instead of personal addresses in public directories.
  • Certificate Transparency Monitoring: Monitor CT logs for unauthorized certificates issued for your domains, a leading indicator of subdomain takeover or rogue infrastructure.
  • Code & Document Hygiene: Audit GitHub/GitLab orgs for secrets; strip metadata from published PDFs and images.
  • Job Posting OpSec: Avoid listing specific security product versions. "Experience with enterprise EDR" is safer than "CrowdStrike Falcon v7.2."
  • Geographic & Hosting Obfuscation: Use global CDNs and avoid hostnames that reveal data-center locations.

Consolidated Adversary TTP → Defensive Control Matrix

Blue Team Note:
Reconnaissance is the only phase where the adversary is more vulnerable than you. They rely on third-party platforms; CT logs, search indexes, social networks that you can monitor. Focus on reducing your attackable footprint rather than trying to hide entirely. An asset that does not exist cannot be fingerprinted.
