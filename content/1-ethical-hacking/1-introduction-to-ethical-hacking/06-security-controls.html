2. Beginner Level

Foundational concepts for practitioners new to defensive architecture and security controls. Assume zero prior knowledge of risk management, CTI, or incident response frameworks.

# Core Concepts

Information security controls are the guard rails that keep an organization’s information assets usable, trustworthy, and available. At a high level we are protecting:

  • Confidentiality: Who is allowed to see the data
  • Integrity: Whether the data is accurate and unchanged
  • Availability: Whether the data and services are reachable when needed

For people interacting with that data, we also care about:

  • Authentication: Proving who you are.
  • Authorization: What you are allowed to do.
  • Non-repudiation: Being unable to deny an approved action later.

Analogy / Context

Think of security controls as the locks, alarms, and guards protecting a bank vault. The locks (technical controls) keep unauthorized people out. The alarms (detective controls) alert when someone tries to break in. The guards (physical controls) respond when an alert is triggered. A bank that only installs locks without alarms or guards is still vulnerable; one with alarms but no locks will spend all its time responding to false alarms. Security controls must work together.

Key Principle: Information is your most valuable asset. Controls, processes, and people all have to work together to keep that asset safe while the business keeps moving.

3. Intermediate Level

Hands-on application of security controls, defense-in-depth architecture, risk management, CTI, and incident response in real environments.

Information Assurance (IA)

Information Assurance is about keeping information trustworthy throughout its life cycle, while it is stored, processed, or transmitted. IA combines technical, physical, and administrative controls to make sure only authorized people (or systems) can access and use information in acceptable ways.

Typical IA Activities

  • Local policy and process design: Writing practical policies and procedures that keep systems at an acceptable security baseline.
  • Network & authentication strategy: Designing segmented networks, strong identity and access controls, and hardened endpoints.
  • Vulnerability and threat identification: Running regular assessments to understand where you are exposed and how an attacker might move.
  • Planning & resourcing: Deciding what people, tools, and budget are needed to close the most important gaps.
  • Certification & Accreditation (C&A): Formally checking that systems meet defined security requirements before they go live.
  • Awareness & training: Making sure everyone who touches information systems understands their responsibilities.
Key Outcome: When IA (Information Assurance) is done properly, you get two big wins: business continuity (the organization can keep operating) and reduced risk (attacks are harder, noisier, and more expensive for an adversary).

Continual / Adaptive Security Strategy

A static security program quickly becomes obsolete. An adaptive security strategy assumes that threats, technology, and the business will keep changing, so your controls must keep evolving as well. A simple way to frame this is: 

Protect → Detect → Respond → Predict
Phase Purpose Typical Activities
Protect Reduce the attack surface up front. Policies, hardening, patching, segmentation, access control, firewalls, endpoint security, secure configuration baselines.
Detect Spot abnormal or malicious activity early. Log collection, SIEM rules, NIDS/HIDS, packet capture, anomaly detection, regular monitoring of key telemetry.
Respond Contain damage and restore normal operations. Incident response playbooks, triage, containment, eradication, recovery, stakeholder communication, lessons learned.
Predict Get ahead of "what's coming next". Risk & vulnerability assessments, attack surface analysis, consumption of threat intelligence, purple-team/emulation exercises.
For Red Teams: The adaptive model tells you where defenders are likely watching.
For Blue Teams: It becomes a simple checklist: Are we actively doing all four phases or just buying "protect" products?

# Defense-in-Depth

Defense-in-depth is the idea that no single control is perfect. Instead, you stack multiple layers so that when one fails, others still stand between the attacker and the crown jewels.

Defensive Layers & Adversary Operations

Perimeter Layer

  • Examples: Firewalls, Web Application Firewalls (WAF), VPN, DDoS Protection.
Adversary Aspect (Entry Target):
"I need to find a port that is open and allowed through the firewall. If I can't brute force it, I'll look for a service vulnerability."

Network Layer

  • Examples: Network Segmentation, Network Access Control (NAC), Internal Firewalls.
Adversary Aspect (Lateral Movement):
"I'm in the DMZ. Can I ping the database server? If not, I need to hop through a web server that has access to both."

Endpoint Layer

  • Examples: Endpoint Detection & Response (EDR), Host Hardening, Application Control.
Adversary Aspect (Stabilization):
"I dropped a shell. Is EDR going to kill my process? I might need to use 'mimikatz' or obfuscate my payload."

Application Layer

  • Examples: Strict Input Validation, Strong Authorization Models, Granular Security Logging.
Adversary Aspect (Exploitation):
"The input field doesn’t sanitize SQL. I can inject `' OR '1'='1` to bypass login."

Data Layer

  • Examples: Data-at-Rest Encryption, Secure Backups, Tokenization Schemes, Data Loss Prevention (DLP).
Adversary Aspect (Exfiltration):
"I have the files. Are they encrypted at rest? If so, do I have the key? If not, I need to steal the key first."
Remeber: A well-designed defense-in-depth strategy slows attackers down and gives defenders more chances to detect, respond, and recover.

# Risk and Risk Management

What is Risk?

In security, risk is the combination of:

  • The likelihood that something bad will happen, and
  • The impact if it actually does.

A common model is:

Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Impact (or Asset Value)

You cannot remove all risk, but you can understand it, prioritize it, and decide what to do about it.

Risk Levels & Risk Matrix

A risk matrix helps you quickly classify risks by combining likelihood and consequence. Below is a simple example; each organization should tune the labels and thresholds to its own context.

Likelihood ↓ / Impact → Insignificant Minor Major Critical
Rare Low Low Medium Medium
Unlikely Low Medium Medium High
Possible Medium Medium High Extreme
Likely Medium High High Extreme
Almost Certain High High Extreme Extreme

This supports the simple exam-style equation:

Level of Risk = Consequence × Likelihood

Risk Management Phases

Risk management is the ongoing process of understanding and controlling risk so that it stays within what the organization considers "acceptable".

Phase Goal Key Outputs
Risk Identification: Discover what could go wrong. List of risks, sources, causes, affected assets, and potential consequences.
Risk Assessment: Understand likelihood and impact. Risk ratings/matrix, prioritised list of what to tackle first.
Risk Treatment: Decide what to do about each risk. Controls to reduce, avoid, transfer, or accept each risk, implementation plans and owners.
Risk Tracking & Review: Check if controls still work. Metrics, audits, updated risk register, lessons learned, improved controls.

# Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI)

Cyber Threat Intelligence is curated, contextual information about adversaries, their capabilities, and the threats they pose. It converts raw data about attacks "in the wild" into something your organization can actually act on.

Types of Threat Intelligence

Type Audience Focus Typical Sources
Strategic: Executives, CISO, senior management Big-picture risk, trends, attacker motives, high-level business impact. OSINT, CTI vendors, ISACs/ISAOs, industry reports, geopolitical analysis.
Tactical: SOC managers, architects, security engineers Adversary TTPs, common tools, infrastructure, and campaigns. Malware and campaign reports, forensic write-ups, vendor advisories.
Operational: Incident responders, threat hunters, forensics teams Specific threat actors and operations targeting your sector or organization. Human intelligence, underground forums, closed communities, law enforcement, social media monitoring.
Technical: SOC analysts, IR teams, automation systems Short-lived Indicators of Compromise (IOCs): IPs, hashes, domains, URLs, C2 patterns. IOC feeds, sandbox output, SIEM/EDR telemetry, vendor data streams.
Advanced CTI programs blend all four types so that strategy, operations, and day-to-day detection all pull in the same direction.

Threat Intelligence Lifecycle

Threat intelligence is not "download a feed and hope for the best". It follows a lifecycle that turns raw data into useful, validated intelligence.

Phase What Happens Key Questions
Planning & Direction: Define requirements, consumers, priorities, and scope of the intelligence program. What do we need to know? For whom? To support which decisions?
Collection: Gather raw data from internal logs and external sources (HUMINT, OSINT, sensors, third-party feeds, etc.). Where will we get this data and how often?
Processing & Exploitation: Normalize, deduplicate, enrich, decrypt, translate, and structure data so that it is usable. How do we convert raw events into consistent, queryable information?
Analysis & Production: Analysts interpret the processed data, identify patterns and threats, and produce finished intelligence (reports, alerts, hunting packages). What does this mean for us? What should we do about it?
Dissemination & Integration: Deliver intelligence to the right people and systems. Integrate IOCs and TTPs into tooling, collect feedback and refine requirements. Did this help? What needs to change for the next cycle?

# Threat Modeling

Threat modeling is a structured way to think about "how could this be attacked?" before and after a system goes live. It forces you to view the system through an attacker's lens, identify weak points, and design controls where they actually matter.

High-Level Goals

  • Identify relevant threats for a specific application or system.
  • Spot design-level vulnerabilities early, before they become expensive.
  • Improve security architecture and justify controls.

Typical Threat Modeling Steps

  1. Identify security objectives: What are we trying to protect and why?
  2. Create an application overview: Components, data flows, trust boundaries, deployment diagram.
  3. Decompose the application: Entry points, exit points, trust transitions, where data is validated or transformed.
  4. Identify threats: Use checklists, STRIDE, or attack libraries to ask, "What could go wrong here?"
  5. Identify vulnerabilities & mitigations: Map threats to specific weaknesses, then design controls and tests.
Keep threat modeling iterative. Revisit it when the design changes, new features arrive, or a fresh class of attack becomes relevant.

# Incident Management

Incident management is the wider process that surrounds security incidents; spotting them, coordinating the response, restoring service, and learning from what happened. Incident response is one piece inside this larger function.

Core Goals of Incident Management

  • Detect and handle incidents quickly and consistently.
  • Limit business impact and downtime.
  • Improve service quality and resilience over time.
  • Increase staff awareness and readiness.

Typical Supporting Activities

  • Vulnerability and artifact analysis.
  • Security awareness training for staff.
  • Monitoring for intrusions and misuse.
  • Coordination across HR, legal, IT, and external providers.

# Incident Handling & Response (IH&R)

Incident handling and response is the hands-on playbook for dealing with a real security incident. It's where all the theory suddenly becomes real, and every decision matters.

When something goes wrong i.e. a compromised workstation, a suspicious outbound connection, ransomware in early stages, an account behaving strangely, the incident response (IR) team steps in to contain the blast radius, preserve evidence, and restore stability.

IR is not just about technical skill. It's about coordination, discipline, and decision-making under pressure. A solid incident response program prevents a minor intrusion from becoming a full-blown organizational crisis.

Step Focus Examples & Adversary Counter-Measures
1. Preparation: Be ready before anything happens. Policies, playbooks, tooling, training, asset inventories, IR team roles.
Adversary: Targeted ransomware groups wait for backup windows to strike.
2. Recording & Assignment: Capture and route the initial report. Tickets, hotline, SOC alerts, first classification and owner assignment.
3. Triage: Understand what you are dealing with. Validate the incident, determine type, scope, severity, and priority.
Adversary: Uses "Living off the Land" (LOLBin) tools to blend in with normal traffic.
4. Notification: Inform the right stakeholders. Management, legal, affected teams, third parties, regulators (if needed).
5. Containment: Stop the bleeding. Isolate hosts, block accounts or IPs, disable services.
Adversary: Disconnects from C2 server to avoid detection during "dormancy".
6. Evidence & Forensics: Preserve and analyze what happened. Log and disk collection, memory images, timeline analysis.
Adversary: Wipes event logs or uses fileless malware to leave no disk traces.
7. Eradication: Remove root cause and backdoors. Patch vulnerabilities, clean or rebuild systems, remove malware.
Adversary: Leaves a second backdoor (e.g., new cron job) if only one is removed.
8. Recovery: Bring systems safely back into production. Restore from backups, validate integrity, increase monitoring.
Adversary: Waits for the network to go quiet before re-infecting.
9. Post-Incident Activities: Capture lessons and close the loop. After-action review, update policies and playbooks.
Adversary: Studies public reports to see if defenders learned their TTPs.

4. Advanced / Operator Level

Deep technical detail for operators conducting authorized assessments, purple-team exercises, or incident-response engagements. This level covers AI/ML in security, AI weaponization, and the architecture of modern defensive programs.

Role of AI and ML in Cyber Security

Modern environments create more data than human analysts can realistically review. Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) help by automating pattern detection, anomaly spotting, and repetitive decision-making. Used correctly, they amplify both offensive and defensive capabilities.

ML Basics

Type Description Security Examples
Supervised Learning: Models train on labelled data ("this is benign", "this is malicious") and learn to classify new samples. Spam/phishing classification, malware family classification, fraud detection.
Unsupervised Learning: Models look for structure in unlabeled data and group similar items. Anomaly detection on network flows, clustering similar alerts or user behaviours.

Where AI/ML Help Defenders

Area How AI / ML Assist
Password & Authentication Security: Behavioural biometrics, adaptive MFA, anomaly detection on login locations, devices and patterns.
Phishing Detection: Classifying emails and URLs, spotting look-alike domains, suspicious content, or attachment behaviour at scale.
Threat & Anomaly Detection: Learning "normal" network or endpoint behaviour, then flagging outliers for analyst review.
Vulnerability Management: Prioritising findings based on exploitability, external threat data, and business context instead of raw CVSS.
Behavioural Analytics: Building user and entity behaviour baselines (UEBA) to detect credential theft and insider threats.
Network Security: Automatically mapping network flows, segmenting assets, and proposing or tuning firewall policies.
AI-Driven AV & EDR: Behaviour-based malware detection rather than static signature matching, catching novel or obfuscated samples.
Fraud & Botnet Detection: Identifying unusual payment patterns, spotting automated traffic, and correlating signals across large volumes of events.
Of course, attackers can also weaponise AI, for better phishing, evasive malware, or automated reconnaissance. Which means defenders must constantly evolve their use of AI as well.

Examples of AI Weaponization

Attackers are not just using AI to write code, they are using it to outpace traditional defenses.

Polymorphic Malware Generation

  • The Attack: Traditional antivirus relies on signatures or digital fingerprints. Changing a single line of code alters the signature, allowing the malware to bypass classic scanners.
  • AI Weaponization: Attackers leverage LLMs to automatically rewrite malware structures, swap variable names, and modify execution logic while maintaining the underlying malicious capabilities.
[Adversary Prompt Input]
"Rewrite this Python ransomware script to use different encryption libraries and obfuscate file paths, but keep the core logic."
Defender Counter-Measure (Behavioral Analysis):
Transition from signature-based tools to real-time behavioral analysis engines. Deploy machine learning models designed to flag any system process that rapidly encrypts hundreds of files, regardless of the binary signature structure.

Hyper-Personalized Phishing (Spear Phishing)

  • The Attack: Classic social engineering lures are generic, mass-distributed, and regularly caught by basic email filters due to structural layout indicators.
  • AI Weaponization: Attackers scrape target OSINT data from public profiles and press releases. The model crafts a highly tailored email mirroring an executive's precise writing style and referencing active internal projects.
[AI-Generated Phishing Lure]
"Hi Sarah, just reviewing the Q3 merger docs we discussed in Berlin last month. Can you approve the attached invoice? Thanks, John."
Defender Counter-Measure (Semantic NLP Monitoring):
Deploy Natural Language Processing sensors to constantly analyze email streams for structural shifts in conversational tone, urgency, or context, flagging anomalies automatically if request profiles do not align with baseline communications.

Automated Vulnerability Discovery

  • The Attack: Manual offensive validation paths are resource-heavy, while standard signature-based scanners are exceptionally noisy and regularly miss deep business logic flaws.
  • AI Weaponization: Automated autonomous bots crawl target footprints, isolate input handlers, and evaluate thousands of injection variants per second. The agent actively parses server response delays or error exceptions to refine subsequent payloads.
[Automated Adversary Action Loop]
Bot evaluates ' OR '1'='1', transitions to admin'--, and automatically generates custom payloads based on database feedback loops to map the SQLi vector in 30 seconds.
Defender Counter-Measure (AI-Driven WAF Rules):
Implement web application firewalls utilizing deep traffic pattern analysis engines to establish dynamic operational baselines and auto-block aggressive, high-speed probing vectors before an exploit payload can be successfully compiled.

5. Adversarial Level

Threat actor perspective. Advanced persistent threats, criminal groups, and red teams do not ignore security controls, they study them to locate the gap between documented policy and ground-truth implementation.

# Threat Actor Behavior

Control Gaps & Bypass Techniques

  • Target organizations that treat security controls as "set and forget" rather than continuously tested and tuned.
  • Exploit compensating control waivers that were accepted "temporarily" but never revisited.
  • Probe for segmentation gaps between less-scoped corporate VLANs and high-security Cardholder Data Environments (CDE).
  • Weaponize AI for polymorphic malware generation, hyper-personalized phishing, and automated vulnerability discovery.

Incident Response Friction

  • Launch attacks during weekends or holidays when IR teams are understaffed and response times are slower.
  • Use "Living off the Land" (LOLBin) tools to blend with normal administrative traffic and delay triage.
  • Leave dormant backdoors that re-infect after the initial eradication phase completes.
  • Study public breach reports to understand defender TTPs and evolve attack patterns accordingly.

AI/ML Exploitation

  • Train models on security vendor documentation to identify detection gaps in popular EDR/AV products.
  • Use LLMs to generate social engineering lures that bypass semantic NLP detection engines.
  • Automate reconnaissance to discover misconfigured assets, exposed APIs, and weak authentication endpoints.
Remember: "Controls do not stop a determined adversary; they slow the adversary down, force them to reveal themselves, and buy defenders time to detect and respond. Your job is to make that time as short as possible."

7. Key Takeaways

Area What Was Proven Operational Meaning
Information Assurance Protecting information throughout its lifecycle requires technical, physical, and administrative controls working together. Supports business continuity while reducing overall organizational risk.
Adaptive Security A static security program becomes obsolete quickly; controls must evolve as threats and technology change. Improves an organization's ability to Protect, Detect, Respond, and Predict emerging threats.
Defense-in-Depth No single control is perfect, and layers must be stacked to compensate for individual failures. Multiple defensive layers increase attacker workload and create additional detection opportunities.
Risk Management Organizations cannot eliminate all risk but can identify, assess, prioritize, and treat it systematically. Resources can be focused on the threats most likely to cause significant impact.
Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) Raw threat data becomes actionable only when curated, contextualized, and aligned to organizational decisions. Improves strategic planning, threat hunting, incident response, and defensive decision-making.
Threat Intelligence Lifecycle Intelligence is most effective when continuously collected, processed, analyzed, and integrated. Creates a repeatable process for understanding and responding to evolving threats.
Threat Modeling Anticipating attacker logic during design prevents expensive vulnerabilities in production. Helps identify design weaknesses and implement controls earlier in the development lifecycle.
Incident Management & Response Preparation, coordination, containment, recovery, and lessons learned are critical during security incidents. Reduces operational disruption and accelerates recovery from attacks.
AI & Machine Learning Automation, anomaly detection, behavioral analysis, and large-scale data processing improve defensive efficiency. Improves security monitoring, threat detection, and defensive efficiency at scale.
AI Weaponization Attackers can use AI for phishing, malware mutation, reconnaissance, and vulnerability discovery. Defenders must evolve beyond traditional signature-based controls toward behavioral and intelligence-driven defenses.
Reminder: Treat controls, CTI, and IR as a single feedback loop. Measure outcomes, iterate playbooks, and prioritise highest-impact mitigations first.
