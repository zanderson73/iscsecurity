1. Executive Summary

Reporting and documentation are core responsibilities in ethical hacking. A penetration test is not complete when a vulnerability is discovered; it is complete when the finding is clearly documented, risk is explained, evidence is preserved, and remediation guidance is delivered to the right audience.

This section introduces professional reporting habits that support red teams, blue teams, purple teams, auditors, executives, and technical remediation teams.

  • Objective 1: Understand why documentation is a critical part of ethical hacking.
  • Objective 2: Learn the structure of a professional security report.
  • Objective 3: Capture evidence, risk, impact, and remediation guidance clearly and ethically.

2. Beginner Level

Ethical hacking is not just about finding weaknesses. It is about communicating those weaknesses in a way that helps an organization understand risk and take action. Good documentation turns technical work into business value.

Core Concepts

  • Authorization: Always document the approved scope, dates, systems, and rules of engagement.
  • Evidence: Record proof of what was discovered without exposing unnecessary sensitive data.
  • Risk: Explain how serious a finding is and why it matters.
  • Impact: Describe what could happen if the issue is exploited.
  • Remediation: Provide practical steps to fix or reduce the risk.
  • Audience: Write for both technical and non-technical readers.

Analogy / Context

Think of an ethical hacker like a building inspector. Finding a broken lock is useful, but the real value comes from writing a clear report that explains where the lock is, why it is dangerous, how it was verified, and how it should be repaired.

Key Principle: If a finding is not documented clearly, it may not be understood, prioritized, or fixed.

3. Intermediate Level

During an authorized engagement, notes should be collected continuously. Waiting until the end of testing often leads to missing commands, incomplete evidence, forgotten timestamps, and weak remediation advice.

Practical Application

A basic ethical hacking report should connect technical evidence to business risk. For example, instead of only writing "SSH is exposed," a stronger report explains which host exposes SSH, whether weak authentication is possible, what evidence supports the observation, what risk this creates, and how the organization should respond.

Basic Reporting Workflow

1. Confirm authorization and scope
2. Take structured notes during testing
3. Capture evidence carefully
4. Validate each finding
5. Assign risk severity
6. Explain business impact
7. Recommend remediation
8. Review the report for accuracy
9. Deliver findings securely
10. Support the remediation discussion

Example Finding Structure

Finding Title:
Weak Password Policy Identified

Affected Asset:
Internal authentication portal

Severity:
Medium

Description:
The application allows short passwords and does not appear to enforce complexity requirements.

Evidence:
Screenshot of password policy behavior and test account result.

Business Impact:
Weak passwords increase the likelihood of account compromise.

Recommendation:
Enforce minimum password length, complexity requirements, account lockout protections, and multi-factor authentication where appropriate.
Key Principle: A report should be reproducible, readable, and actionable.

4. Advanced / Operator Level

Mature reporting requires more than listing vulnerabilities. Operators must document methodology, preserve evidence integrity, protect sensitive data, and communicate risk in a way that supports decision-making.

Operational Considerations

  • Scope control: Clearly distinguish in-scope systems from out-of-scope observations.
  • Evidence handling: Store screenshots, logs, and command output securely.
  • Data minimization: Avoid including passwords, private keys, tokens, personal data, or customer records unless absolutely necessary.
  • Timeline accuracy: Record dates, times, time zones, and major testing actions.
  • Reproducibility: Include enough detail for the client to verify the issue without exposing exploit paths unnecessarily.
  • Remediation quality: Recommend practical fixes, not vague advice.
  • Executive clarity: Translate technical weaknesses into operational, financial, legal, or reputational risk.

Report Sections Used in Professional Engagements

  • Cover Page
  • Confidentiality Notice
  • Executive Summary
  • Scope and Rules of Engagement
  • Methodology
  • Summary of Findings
  • Detailed Technical Findings
  • Risk Rating Methodology
  • Remediation Roadmap
  • Appendices and Evidence
Operator Note: A professional report should help the client fix risk, not impress them with jargon.

5. Adversarial Level

Threat actors do not write client reports, but they do keep operational notes. They track targets, credentials, exposed services, exploitable weaknesses, and successful attack paths. Ethical hackers document similar observations, but with authorization, restraint, and the goal of improving security.

Threat Actor Behavior

Attackers often maintain target profiles, infrastructure notes, access records, and privilege escalation paths. Ethical reports should help defenders understand how an attacker could move from initial discovery to real-world impact.

Defensive Translation

A good ethical hacking report converts attacker behavior into defensive action. For example, if an attacker could abuse exposed remote access, the report should explain how to restrict access, enforce strong authentication, monitor login attempts, and reduce external exposure.

Adversarial Mindset: Document the path an attacker would take, then give defenders the information needed to break that path.

7. Reporting and Documentation Applications

Ethical hackers use documentation tools to organize notes, screenshots, commands, evidence, findings, and remediation steps. The best tool depends on the engagement type, team workflow, and reporting requirements.

Application Best For Strengths Typical Use
CherryTree Structured pentest notes Tree-style notes, code blocks, screenshots, local storage Engagement notes, evidence organization, command history
Obsidian Knowledge bases and linked notes Markdown, backlinks, graph view, plugins Methodology notes, reusable checklists, research vaults
Joplin Encrypted note-taking Markdown, sync support, encryption options Secure notes, engagement journals, client-specific records
KeepNote Legacy pentest note-taking Simple notebook structure Older lab environments and basic evidence notes
Dradis Team-based reporting Collaboration, templates, findings management Professional pentest reports and multi-tester engagements
Serpico Report generation Finding templates, report automation Generating repeatable assessment reports

CherryTree

CherryTree is a popular hierarchical note-taking application often used by ethical hackers because it allows testers to organize notes by phase, host, finding, evidence, and commands.

Install on Debian / Ubuntu / Kali

sudo apt update
sudo apt install cherrytree -y

Basic Usage

Example CherryTree Structure:

Engagement Name
├── 00 - Scope and Authorization
├── 01 - Reconnaissance
├── 02 - Scanning
├── 03 - Enumeration
├── 04 - Findings
│   ├── EH-001 - Exposed Admin Login
│   ├── EH-002 - Weak Password Policy
│   └── EH-003 - Missing Security Headers
├── 05 - Evidence
└── 06 - Final Report Notes

CherryTree works well for local note-taking. It supports formatted text, code blocks, tables, images, and file attachments. Students should create one notebook per engagement and avoid mixing client data between projects.

CherryTree Tip: Use a consistent folder structure for every engagement. Consistency makes reporting faster and reduces the chance of missing evidence.

Obsidian

Obsidian is a Markdown-based note-taking application. It is useful for building a long-term ethical hacking knowledge base because notes can be linked together.

Install on Linux using AppImage

# Download the Obsidian AppImage from the official website
# Then make it executable:
chmod +x Obsidian*.AppImage

# Run it:
./Obsidian*.AppImage

Basic Usage

Example Obsidian Vault:

Ethical-Hacking-Vault
├── Methodology
├── Checklists
├── Reporting Templates
├── Findings Library
├── OSINT Notes
├── Scanning Notes
└── Remediation References

Obsidian is especially useful for reusable methodology notes, personal checklists, and linking related topics such as OSINT, scanning, enumeration, exploitation, and remediation.

Obsidian Tip: Use Obsidian for your personal knowledge base, but be careful when storing sensitive client data in plugin-enabled or synced vaults.

Joplin

Joplin is a note-taking application that supports Markdown and encryption features. It can be useful for students and professionals who want organized notes with optional synchronization.

Install on Linux

# Install using the official install script
wget -O - https://raw.githubusercontent.com/laurent22/joplin/dev/Joplin_install_and_update.sh | bash

Basic Usage

Example Joplin Notebooks:

Pentest Engagement
├── Scope
├── Daily Notes
├── Evidence
├── Findings
├── Remediation
└── Debrief Notes

Joplin is useful for maintaining Markdown notes and organizing them into notebooks. When using synchronization, testers should ensure storage locations are approved by the client or organization.

Joplin Tip: Enable encryption when storing sensitive notes, and never sync client data to unapproved personal cloud accounts.

Dradis

Dradis is designed for professional security teams that need collaborative reporting. It helps testers manage findings, evidence, notes, and report templates in one place.

Install with Docker

# Example Docker-based setup
docker pull dradis/dradis-ce
docker run -it -p 3000:3000 dradis/dradis-ce

Basic Usage

Typical Dradis Workflow:

1. Create a project
2. Add engagement scope
3. Add hosts or affected assets
4. Import notes or tool output
5. Create findings
6. Attach evidence
7. Generate a report from a template

Dradis is better suited to team environments than solo note-taking. It is useful when multiple testers need to contribute to the same engagement report.

Dradis Tip: Use standardized finding templates so every tester reports severity, impact, evidence, and remediation consistently.

Faraday

Faraday is a collaborative platform for managing security assessment data. It can help organize vulnerabilities, assets, tool outputs, and remediation status.

Install with Docker Compose

# Clone the Faraday Community repository
git clone https://github.com/infobyte/faraday.git

# Enter the directory
cd faraday

# Start using Docker Compose
docker compose up

Basic Usage

Typical Faraday Workflow:

1. Create a workspace
2. Add assets
3. Import scan results
4. Review discovered issues
5. Validate findings
6. Assign severity
7. Track remediation progress

Faraday is useful for larger assessments where findings need to be tracked across many hosts, services, and testers.

Faraday Tip: Imported scanner results should always be manually validated before being included in a final report.

Markdown and Git

Markdown files stored in Git repositories provide a simple, transparent, and version-controlled reporting workflow. This approach is especially useful for technical teams.

Install Git

sudo apt update
sudo apt install git -y

Basic Usage

mkdir ethical-hacking-report
cd ethical-hacking-report

git init

mkdir scope notes evidence findings report
touch README.md
touch findings/EH-001-example-finding.md

git add .
git commit -m "Initial report structure"

Example Markdown Finding

# EH-001 - Exposed Administrative Login Page

## Severity
Medium

## Affected Asset
lab-web-01

## Description
An administrative login page was accessible from an untrusted network segment.

## Evidence
Screenshot stored in: evidence/EH-001/admin-login.png

## Business Impact
Exposed administrative interfaces increase the attack surface and may support brute-force or credential-stuffing attempts.

## Recommendation
Restrict access to trusted networks, enforce multi-factor authentication, and monitor failed login attempts.
Markdown Tip: Markdown is simple, portable, and easy to review, but sensitive repositories must be stored securely.

Scenario Recommended Tool Reason
Beginner lab notes CherryTree Simple structure and easy evidence organization

8. Key Takeaways

  • Reporting is part of the mission: Ethical hacking is incomplete without clear, accurate documentation.
  • Evidence must be handled carefully: Capture enough proof to validate risk, but avoid unnecessary exposure of sensitive data.
  • Reports must drive action: Every finding should explain the issue, impact, severity, and remediation path.
  • Different audiences need different detail: Executives need risk and impact; technical teams need evidence and remediation steps.
  • Good notes create better reports: Document continuously throughout the engagement.
Reminder: A vulnerability that is poorly explained is a vulnerability that may never get fixed.

9. Assessment & Next Steps

Knowledge Check

  1. Why is reporting considered a core part of ethical hacking?
  2. What is the difference between technical evidence and business impact?
  3. Why should sensitive information be minimized in a report?
  4. What information should be included in a finding?
  5. Who are the main audiences for a penetration testing report?

Lab Exercises

  • Lab 1: Create a mock engagement folder with sections for scope, evidence, notes, screenshots, and findings.
  • Lab 2: Write a one-page finding using a fictional vulnerability from a training lab.
  • Lab 3: Rewrite a technical finding for two audiences: an executive reader and a system administrator.
  • Lab 4: Create a findings register with severity, affected asset, evidence location, remediation owner, and status.

Recommended Reading

  • OWASP Web Security Testing Guide: Useful for understanding structured web application testing and documentation.
  • OWASP Top 10: Helps connect common vulnerabilities to business risk and remediation guidance.
  • CVSS: Common framework for understanding vulnerability severity scoring.
  • MITRE ATT&CK: Helps map attacker behavior to defensive controls and reporting language.
Reporting Reminder: A finding is only valuable if it can be understood, verified, and acted upon. Clear evidence, accurate documentation, and practical remediation are the hallmarks of a professional ethical hacker.
